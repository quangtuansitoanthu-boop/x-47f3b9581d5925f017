TITLE: 02. Author's Preface
TOMTAT: Layman Guoqing recounts his journey from atheism to taking refuge in the Dharma after a trip to Mount Wutai, where he met an elderly monk, and his vow to record true stories of cause and effect to share with readers.
-----
Author's Preface

How I came to the faith

I was born in 1944, and since childhood I had received an atheist education, so I always regarded religion as superstition. How, then, did I enter the path of studying Buddhism? It all began with a summer excursion to Mount Wutai more than a dozen years ago.

Mount Wutai in Shanxi Province is one of the four great holy Buddhist sites of China, revered as the bodhimanda of Manjushri Bodhisattva. On the mountain stand pagodas majestic and solemn, ancient and renowned for a thousand years, which I wandered through. In the Great Hero Hall, incense smoke curls unceasingly, and even the reverberation of the great bronze bell makes the listener feel light and uplifted, worldly cares melting away. The scenery here always moved me with an indescribable stirring, as though I had known this place long, long ago.

On my second excursion to Mount Wutai, I was in high spirits and suddenly conceived the idea of seeking out the secluded hermitages of ascetic monks, for few know anything of their daily lives. I remember that morning forever: the air was biting cold, mist hung thick on all sides. Eagerly I put my pack in order, carefully bringing a map, and set out alone to explore the quiet splendors. Little did I know that this very adventure would change my life — and the lives of many others afterward. To this day I regard Mount Wutai as the land where I was born a second time.

Setting out, I deliberately avoided the level roads and chose only the winding, twisting paths. Along the way, looking up I saw only towering peaks, deep dark trees, drifting mist, and glinting snow. I felt my spirits freshened and my mind emptied. The road grew ever more rugged. I followed a mountain stream, savoring nature's painting of "ancient trees and cold cliffs" — stones breathing chill, primeval trees soaring, lush foliage; blue sky, white clouds, a road full of strange herbs and wondrous flowers. Enchanted by the outer scene, I walked on entranced, forgetting all worldly matters, immersed in green waters and lovely hills.

Absorbed in climbing passes and fording streams, I forgot that midday was near, yet by then I had still seen no trace of any ascetic monk, and my heart could not help a touch of disappointment. Just as I was looking around in bewilderment, suddenly from afar came the sound of a wooden bell: "Toc! Toc! Toc!"... Its ancient lingering resonance drifted somewhere through the mountains, its echo rising from a valley, carried from a deep dark forest to the west.

My spirits revived; I hurried toward the valley. Turning into a deep, secluded yet lovely path, above me shimmering veils of violet cloud, trees green and fresh like a fairy realm. By now the bell sounded slower, then stopped altogether. A clear blue stream wound without a speck of dust before my eyes, its spray fine as pearls. I bent down to scoop up water and drink; its cool, pure flavor fragranced my whole mouth. Drinking my fill, I washed my face and splashed water over it with my hands. Suddenly I noticed someone standing by the stream, and I raised my head.

An old monk, beard and hair unshaven, patched robe and straw sandals, stood there with arms folded. A passing breeze stirred his beard and hair, his robe fluttering; our eyes met for an instant, yet I felt dizzy, my senses dazed. How pure and kind the monk's eyes were. I felt he was so familiar, like a parent from a former life; he radiated a powerful attraction that unsettled my body and mind...

"Almsman, forgive me for disturbing you!" the monk said, palms joined.
"Not at all! It is I who disturb Your reverend solitude," I replied in confusion.
"Does the almsman enjoy this quiet place?"
"Yes, I only wished to escape the noise for a while, so I walk alone admiring the scenery." (In truth I meant nothing like what I said, for I was secretly longing for just such a wondrous encounter!)

"Well, I have disturbed you all the same. Wander on as you please; this old monk goes ahead!"

Before I had recovered from my surprise, the old monk had already glided gently westward. I hurried after him and shyly confessed:
"Venerable, please halt your steps. In truth I have been longing to meet an eminent monk like you..."

"How good! I am no 'eminent monk,' only a clumsy rustic guiding monk."

"Was it you striking the bell just now?" I asked.
The monk replied serenely:
"The bell's sound wakes the worldly traveler's heart;
The clear blue water washes the dusty mind."

I silently pondered the monk's words, and in a moment I had followed him to a bright, open expanse full of pear trees. The trunks were huge, the branches laden with fruit. To the north the ground rose slightly, and before us stood a small thatched hut. My heart rejoiced with the thought: "I have met an eminent monk in pure retreat, transcendent and world-renouncing."

"The Saha world is no place to hide long; time is short — do not wait for death to begin practice. Fame and gain are but emptiness; worldly love must part; vengeance begets vengeance without end..."

The monk chanted as if idly, yet hearing it, my heart was secretly shaken.

"People in this world only create enmity and never repent; they only enjoy blessings and never accumulate them. Little do they know: one recitation of the Buddha's name increases blessings immeasurably; one prostration before the Buddha extinguishes sins countless as the Ganges' sands!"

Hearing this, I could restrain myself no longer. I stepped forward and knelt before the old monk, pleading urgently:
"Master, please accept me as your disciple. I wish to follow you and learn the Buddha Dharma."

"The Buddha Dharma is like the sea; only faith can enter it. Do you have 'faith'?"
The monk deliberately stressed the word "faith."

This question left me with no reply. Alas! Until now I had always deemed Buddhism superstition, scorning and ignoring all that worship of Buddhas and bodhisattvas... Why today should I devoutly kneel at the feet of a monk I had never met, begging to be his disciple?

For a moment I could hardly answer. Yet "emptiness, worldly dust, repentance"... these words kept ringing, sinking ever deeper into my mind, shaking my heart; within me there suddenly rose a great feeling, as though from very long ago.

As I stood bewildered, my spirit like a masterless thing, I suddenly saw the old monk gazing at me with compassion and attention; in an instant a hundred thousand emotions crossed my heart, and tears fell one after another, streaming down and wetting the old monk's spotless white socks. Though I felt it most rude and strange, I could not stop the gushing spring of tears; I was exactly like a child who has done wrong, unable to speak, knowing only to weep and weep.

"Faith is the mother of all virtues, the source of the Way. But faith is of two kinds: 'right faith' and 'blind faith.' 'Right faith' means right, not deviant; awakened, not deluded. Whoever takes refuge in Buddhism must first establish correct views. 'One bows to the Buddha out of reverence for the Buddha's virtue; one recites the Buddha's name out of gratitude to the Buddha; one reads the sutras to understand their meaning; one sits in meditation to realize the Buddha's state. Whoever realizes it attains the Buddha's Way..."

Like a starving man given food, a thirsty man given drink, I listened to each jeweled word of his teaching, feeling as though every blade of grass, every tree before my eyes radiated dazzling golden light. Reverently I listened on, never noticing the sun sink toward the west.

When he saw me off, the monk took a pear kept in a rock hollow in the stream and handed it to me. I had eaten neither breakfast nor lunch that day. Grateful, I took the pear and bit into it; its cool fragrance chilled my very teeth, clearing my lungs and soothing my heart — I marveled, as though I had eaten a fairy's fruit. Watching me eat with such relish, the monk's compassionate face broke into a faint smile, a profound joyful smile that touched my inmost heart, giving me the feeling that we had known each other closely for a very long time.

"This pear was by nature bitter, hard to swallow, but soaked in the stream water for three full months, it turned sweet. This stream is warm in winter and cool in summer; pears can be stored in its current all year."

"Then you can practice here all year round?" I asked curiously. The monk smiled without denying it.

"The ancient moon like frost, the pure wind like water"... The little stream murmured on. Monk and I walked and talked on the way back. Before I felt cold or weariness, we had reached the rough outer road. I joined my palms and bowed farewell. My heart overflowed with clinging, unwilling to part from him... yet I knew more words were useless.

As if reading my heart, the monk said softly:
"With affinity, we shall meet."

His form melted like the "ancient moon" into the forest. My consolation was that the monk had said he would soon end his closed retreat and begin "entering the world to save beings, teaching according to affinity." Thinking of this, my heart brimmed with joy. I quickened my steps homeward, feeling that if I walked faster I would see him again that much sooner.

Back in the hall, I sat quietly in the guest room, recalling the dreamlike meeting just past, savoring its wonder. Suddenly I remembered I had never asked the monk his name, and my heart sank with endless regret. I only hoped I would soon receive the blessed rain of his Dharma.

Not long after, I reached the monk. From then on, master and disciple were deeply connected, and I was often close to him.

Time slips away; more than a dozen years have flashed by. Now, near sixty, I see the monk devoting his whole heart to the Dharma, forgetting himself, teaching countless beings. As for me, I still drift vainly through the days, unable to let go of everything and so hardly tasting true benefit — how shameful!

Even so, I now refuse to let these vivid, meaning-filled stories be lost, refuse to let these true and precious lessons in cause and effect be buried at the bottom of a grave. So after long hesitation and deliberation, I took up my pen and recorded these true stories to share with readers.

If I do not state clearly the names and places of the real people mentioned in this book, it is because I judge it inconvenient. I beg your understanding.

Moreover, some wording in the book may no longer suit the present time, since old place names and names remain. I only hope that venerable elders and Dharma friends will kindly correct them for me.

I wish you all growth in discipline, concentration, and wisdom, cultivation of both merit and wisdom together, and swift attainment of the Buddha Way.

COMMENTARY:
The author's preface is a moving story of transformation — from an atheist who scorned religion to taking refuge in the Buddha Dharma after being taught by the old monk at Mount Wutai. The image of the bitter pear soaked three months in stream water until it turned sweet is a profound metaphor: however hard and deluded the mind at first, meeting a good spiritual friend and persevering in practice can transform it into the sweetness of awakening. Layman Guoqing uses his own life experience to prove that "with affinity, we shall meet," and that recording these stories of cause and effect is his way of repaying his master and benefiting beings.
