HA QUOC XUONG

Layman Ha Quoc Xuong lived in the Republic of China, a native of Pingxiang, Jiangxi. Grandfather and father both came from official careers, famed for integrity. Quoc Xuong served as district magistrate in several postings; after the revolution he became civil governor of his home province.

In the autumn of the second year of the Republic, implicated in the campaign against Yuan Shikai, the plot failing, he fled and hid in a temple on the Southern Peak in Hunan. From then on Quoc Xuong changed his name to Hoanh Tieu, daily reciting the Shurangama and Cundi mantras and the Buddha's name. In the spring of the fourth year of the Republic, recommended by a person of influence, he was falsely accused by a magistrate and had to go in person to Beijing to clear his name.

At that time Layman La Kiet shared Quoc Xuong's plight. After the calamity the two grew intimate, visiting often. Both kept lifelong vegetarian diet and practiced meditation together. One day in quiet absorption, Quoc Xuong suddenly heard someone call him Lieu Khong Tu. Moved, he composed a verse with these two lines:

Lieu Khong, knowing the road of coming and going,

In this floating world, once more at play!

From then on he turned to the Pure Land, daily reciting several chapters of the Avatamsaka Sutra, bowing one hundred prostrations, and reciting Amitabha's sacred name twenty thousand times. He further composed rules — Keeping Vegetarian Diet, Buddha-Recitation, Mind-Contemplation, Simplicity — to admonish and urge himself.

In the spring of the eighth year of the Republic, Quoc Xuong returned to visit his homeland during a long drought, performed the rain-praying rite, and gained abundant rain filling the fields with water. He then established a feeding altar, reciting sutras to deliver ancestral spirits and the war dead of the North-South conflict. During the offering, all present beheld Amitabha manifest amid the sky, countless hungry ghosts and lonely spirits bowing in homage, then following the Buddha flying to the West. After the work, the layman bade his villagers farewell, saying he would not return. He charged his kin, upon hearing of his passing, not to kill living beings in sacrifice. Returning to Beijing, he fell ill. Family and friends wished to call good physicians, but he declined, saying: "My mind is most peaceful; the road coming and going is clearly known — what need of medicine!". He still strove in worship and recitation as usual, saying: "Now, whether eyes open or closed, I see Buddhas and bodhisattvas manifest in great bodies filling the sky!". Then the illness seemed to ease. When relatives heard and gathered in full, the layman sat upright, slowly gazed round at all standing about, smiled, joined his palms, and quietly passed away.

When the layman first resolved upon Buddha-recitation, he dreamed of lotus blossoms filling the sky, each bearing a seated person, with but one throne vacant. Asked why, one seated nearby answered: "This lotus throne is reserved for Lieu Khong Tu!". And when Quoc Xuong returned to Beijing, his daughter Ha Vinh dreamed she saw her father seated upon a lotus flying to the West. Though a layman in white, he kept the precepts most strictly. As the end neared, his disciple Banh Hien asked for instruction; he said: "We live deep in the Dharma-ending age, and our past karma is very heavy. To sever the root of birth, death, and rebirth, we must recite the Buddha's name and seek the Land of Bliss. In the Pure Land gate: vows must be earnest, repentance most sincere, precepts firmly kept, recitation diligent. The precepts are supreme enlightenment, the root of myriad goods, the foundation of the true teaching, the entry of learners, a good teacher's child. If you keep the three karmas pure while reciting, you shall surely behold Amitabha.".

These words show how strictly he kept precepts and practice.
