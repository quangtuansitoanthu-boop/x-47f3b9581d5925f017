{align:justify|THAI MINH THONG}
{align:justify|Not long ago, Mr. Thai Minh Thong told me his family's true story of retribution:}
{align:justify|Minh Thong's great-grandfather was very rich, with vast fields, raising buffaloes for plowing.}
{align:justify|One day the great-grandfather was ringing a calf's nose; in pain it lashed out, striking a fatal spot, and its master died. Minh Thong's grandfather, seeing his father die tragically, blazed with rage, seized a mallet, and resolved to beat the young buffalo to death.}
{align:justify|The mother buffalo, seeing her master wield a weapon, hastily knelt, bellowing in grief, tears streaming from her eyes, pleading with him not to kill her calf—but the grandfather's rage burned uncontrollably, so he pounded the mallet again and again onto the calf's head until blood splattered and the calf dropped to the ground… The grandfather then hacked off its legs, hung the body on a tree, and—though the buffalo still lived—flayed it, slicing off flesh piece by piece, tormenting it to the utmost. Since local farmers eat no buffalo meat, he just sliced the flesh and flung it about the ground, leaving it to rot and breed maggots. Tortured and abused in agony, the buffalo endured this pain for three days before dying.}
{align:justify|Afterward, the grandfather dreamed a small child spoke to him:}
{align:justify|You made me part early from my mother's love, then cruelly flayed me, sliced my flesh… tormenting me three days before I died. I vow revenge, so that three generations of your line shall know no peace, suffering "house wrecked, line destroyed."}
{align:justify|Later the grandfather married and had a son (Minh Thong's father); when the boy turned three, his wife died. Three years after, the grandfather remarried.}
{align:justify|Minh Thong's father grew up, married, and fathered Minh Thong. When Minh Thong turned three, his mother left home for no reason. At six, his father remarried—so both generations of the Thais early lost their birth mothers and suffered stepmothers' abuse.}
{align:justify|Today, grandparents, parents—all have passed away. After marrying, Minh Thong fathered two children; but when the elder was three and the younger one, his tender marriage suddenly broke apart—when the elder turned six, he divorced his former wife and married his present one.}
{align:justify|As a boy, Minh Thong was struck hard on the head by his younger brother, gravely wounded. The dent remains. Ordinary people walk with straight shins and thighs, but whenever he walks, the cartilage at his knee juts out, knocking with each step—so the bone had to be surgically removed. Worse, serving in the army he developed belly pain; the doctor misdiagnosed "appendicitis—operate at once." But the injection accidentally damaged a spinal nerve, leaving him unable to bend and hardly able to stand long.}
{align:justify|Discharged, he then broke his leg in an accident; a steel pin inside now makes movement harder still. Nor is that all: before sleep, his wife must always pound his leg with a mallet before he can sleep—every single day.}
{align:justify|Minh Thong affirms: I am my grandfather reborn—my head gravely struck, my belly wrongly cut in pain, every night enduring the mallet pounding my leg to sleep—all repaying retribution!…}
{align:justify|This year Thai Minh Thong is thirty-one; attending a Buddha-recitation retreat he received a wondrous response, recalling former lives, which he recounted to me.}
{align:justify|Therefore, do not kill—release captive animals, and give rise to kindness aiding the poor and wretched. If we help all beings live in peace, our own lives shall be peaceful too.}
{align:justify|Told by Master Hue Tham, October 30, 1982 (translated from Tu Van Magazine, vol. 7)}
{align:justify|SNOW IN JUNE}
{align:justify|I always thought this tale a famous fictional script by Quan Hán Khanh—never imagining it was true.}
{align:justify|Seven or eight years ago, through a monastic at the City of Ten Thousand Buddhas, I visited Taiwan and heard Professor Truong recount in detail the strange events she lived through.}
{align:justify|Thirty years ago she did charity work in Taiwan, specializing in counseling—a pioneer lifting society's better trends—so Taiwan's charity workers all call her "Professor Truong." I know her real name, but here we shall call her Professor Truong.}
{align:justify|Today Professor Truong lives in America; I have spoken with her by phone. She is a lay practitioner, lifelong vegetarian, careful in speech, devoted to the Great Compassion Mantra and Buddha-recitation, traveling widely to spread the teachings. She is past seventy.}
{align:justify|Before telling her story, Professor Truong began:}
{align:justify|"What happened to me includes much that is uncanny—I think not everyone meets what I met. But just because you never met such things, do not hastily conclude they are wild fictions!}
{align:justify|Hearing my story, judge it as you will—I dare not object. But I tell it wholly truthfully, never uttering falsehood—on this point, trust me."}
{align:justify|This is a superb story of karmic retribution that I was fortunate to hear from Professor Truong's own lips; those holding high office, wielding power of life and death, should also read it—most beneficial. Now I record it to share with you:}
{align:justify|"Professor Truong entered university late (bearing children before sitting the exams), graduating at thirty-three (by then mother of three sons).}
{align:justify|At thirty-three, in May she found herself pregnant, expecting to deliver exactly on her thirty-fourth birthday. When her mother learned of it, she firmly demanded an abortion. Seeing her mother—long a Buddhist believer, vegetarian for years, strictly keeping the no-killing precept—now demanding her abortion, Mrs. Truong found it most strange; however she pressed, her mother would not explain why.}
{align:justify|During the pregnancy, most strange phenomena occurred. First: such discomfort inside that she must constantly bang her head against the wall. Second: when retching, she demanded her husband and three sons beat her, forcing them to strike with all their might (only then did she feel relief). If husband and sons refused, she turned on them. In former pregnancies she had never suffered anything like this.}
{align:justify|In the fourth month, her mother's friend—a monastic in Taichung—coming to Taipei on business, stopped by the house. Each meeting this monastic, Professor Truong would bow. This time, having bowed, she asked to bow once more.}
{align:justify|The master asked:}
{align:justify|"Why bow to me once more?"}
{align:justify|"I bow on behalf of the infant in my womb."}
{align:justify|The master said joyfully:}
{align:justify|"Very well—I accept the child. Since you wish the little one as my disciple, should anything happen hereafter, remember to tell me."}
{align:justify|In the sixth month, one day Professor Truong's mother craved a fine vegetarian dish sold only at a large Taipei restaurant. A most filial daughter, always wishing to please her mother, she exerted herself to go buy it. Leaving home, boarding the bus—strangely, though all aboard saw her great bulging belly, no one would yield a seat; some even feigned closed eyes to avoid seeing, and no one spared her a thought.}
{align:justify|So Professor Truong had to stand throughout. Suddenly the bus hit something heavy and braked hard, throwing her down and disturbing the pregnancy; she was rushed to emergency care.}
{align:justify|That night her belly ached violently; though the fetus was barely past six months, she delivered prematurely. Though still tiny, the babe was plainly a most beautiful, handsome boy.}
{align:justify|The doctors said the infant came far too early; they had done their utmost, but whether the babe could live they dared not promise—so Professor Truong should not hope too much…}
{align:justify|Professor Truong blamed herself bitterly, angry at not staying quietly home so the child might be born in peace. Now the babe could only lie in an incubator; she hoped the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas would bless and save him. Suddenly she remembered the master who had visited not long before, and telephoned to report:}
{align:justify|"Master, I have delivered—the baby is far too small. But I hope he may be saved."}
{align:justify|The master asked:}
{align:justify|"You wish to save him? Good! Then do as I require. From today, keep a hundred days' vegetarianism. What sutra do you usually recite?"}
{align:justify|"I specialize in the 'White-Robed Mantra.'" }
{align:justify|"Very well—recite this mantra, but ten thousand rounds, mind—if you wish to save the child, hang up and recite at once!"}
{align:justify|In the hospital, hearing her master's instruction, Professor Truong at once recited the White-Robed Mantra without pause. Except sleeping and resting, all remaining time she recited throughout.}
{align:justify|Before long the hospital discharged her. Home again, daily she recited the mantra before the Buddhas. Every day the same.}
{align:justify|When the babe turned one month old, that night she dreamed an evil dream: in dream the child kept dragging her, and she pulled back. Suddenly she let go; the child's face turned pitch black. She woke with a start, thinking the omen surely boded ill, and told her husband to telephone the hospital to ask how the child fared. It was three in the morning. Her husband hesitated to call—but as he wavered, the hospital called to report the babe had just died at three.}
{align:justify|Hearing it, Professor Truong burst into tears.}
{align:justify|Morning came, past six, and the master from Taichung hired a car to their house. Entering, she said at once:}
{align:justify|"Congratulations!"}
{align:justify|Bewildered, Professor Truong thought: "My little boy just died—why congratulations?"…}
{align:justify|But the master asked:}
{align:justify|"Did the infant pass at three this morning?"}
{align:justify|Amazed, Professor Truong said:}
{align:justify|"How do you know?"}
{align:justify|The master told the whole cause:}
{align:justify|"Last night (near midnight), the child came to bid me farewell. He said: I meant to take her away, but now I cannot seize her life. For toward her mother she is wholly filial—her mother craved a dish, and though great with child she strove to buy it for her mother, meeting the bus accident—leaving me too weak to live in her womb. I meant, exactly on her thirty-fourth birthday, to take her.}
{align:justify|The master asked:}
{align:justify|"Between you and her, what enmity is this?"}
{align:justify|The babe answered:}
{align:justify|"An age-old feud. Many lives ago she was a corrupt prefect who wrongly judged my case, and I died unjustly. So from her conception I began tormenting her—repaying the torture I suffered in prison. Making her butt her head against walls, having husband and sons… beat her, was washing away the hatred of the cruel torments I endured in jail."}
{align:justify|But now, since bearing me, she never ceases reciting the Buddha's name; through her recitation, Buddhas and Bodhisattvas ever shelter and bless her—so I cannot approach, with no chance to take her. Yet unreconciled, I ever wait. But her house holds a Buddha hall—I cannot enter at all, for the Buddha images ever guard and shield her—so now I lack the strength; wanting to drag her away, I cannot—so I must leave."}
{align:justify|The Dharma master told Professor Truong not to grieve for this child—he came for revenge, to seize her life and take her away…}
{align:justify|Hearing this, Professor Truong's mother at last revealed the family's hidden matter:}
{align:justify|Professor Truong's lineage held something most strange. Her grandfather, great-grandfather, and father all died exactly on their thirty-sixth birthdays. This ran through five straight generations: the line's eldest sons could never live past thirty-six—their fate, exactly on the thirty-sixth birthday, to leave the world. As for daughters, each eldest daughter died on her thirty-fourth birthday—none escaping…}
{align:justify|Though her mother knew this full well, she dared not tell Professor Truong, fearing to agitate her child—for Professor Truong too is an eldest daughter.}
{align:justify|This is why Professor Truong's mother firmly demanded the abortion.}
