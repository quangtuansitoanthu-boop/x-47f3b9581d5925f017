DUONG LIEN HANG

Layman Duong Lien Hang lived in the Republic of China, a native of Yuyao county, Zhejiang. Poor, he traded from childhood, courteous and capable beyond others.

In the eleventh year of the Republic, Lien Hang learned that his neighbor Dong Giac Hang practiced the pure karma; pleased at heart, he often visited to hear instruction. He then received the refuges as a lay Buddhist. Though weak in letters, in the sutras' meaning his understanding surpassed others. In the ninth month of the Guihai year, friends of the lotus society gathered in common vow, arousing the bodhi-mind; Lien Hang joined that rite.

Late in the spring of the Jiayi year, through illness the layman secretly broke the killing precept by eating meat, thereby gradually estranging himself from his fellow practitioners. By the seventh month his illness worsened; visiting fellow practitioners all said he would surely die. Lien Hang himself judged he could hardly live, and, reflecting, repented what he had done. Early in the eighth month, struggling through pain before the Buddha altar, he wholeheartedly confessed with utmost sincerity, vowing to keep the five precepts and never transgress again. From then on he cast off myriad involvements, severed craving, silently recited the Buddha's sacred name with single mind, and awaited life's end. Knowing his recitation shallow, fellow practitioners six days before his passing invited someone to support his recitation; in the final days fellow practitioners themselves came to chant in aid.

The supporting recitation began on the twelfth day of the eighth month. Past the full moon, Lien Hang suddenly felt spirit and energy light and fresh. On the seventeenth, he said he had dreamed of a radiance like five or six electric lamps blazing. Toward evening his expression seemed as usual; the fellow practitioners judged the hour not yet come and continued reciting into the second watch, preparing to leave. They did not know that the supporting recitation had then reached its most pure and potent point.

Then, hearing the recitation suddenly fall silent, Lien Hang said: "I have not yet reached the West; I beg my fellow practitioners to support me through this night!". Hearing something unusual in his words, his companions raised their voices anew. Within half an hour, the layman suddenly smiled and said: "I have reached the West. Oh! How vast the jeweled pond! See! How beautiful the lotuses! How beautiful the radiance!". He bade the assembly chant loudly in support without pause. Until then Lien Hang had lain silent and still. Now hands and head moved; his mouth repeatedly cried aloud: "Oh! How fresh, wondrous, and beautiful the lotuses! How bright and fair the jeweled pond!". His face beamed with joy, like one surprised by happiness beyond hope. After an hour thus, he fell silent again, hands and feet unmoving, lying on his back gazing fixedly at the Buddha image before his bed. Then his pupils gradually dimmed and his breathing steadily weakened. At five in the morning of the eighteenth, he breathed his last.

That night only four fellow practitioners, in turn chanting loudly through the night, interspersed occasional exhortations. So they continued until an hour and a half after his passing, when newly arrived members took their turn. Support continued until the body of the departed had wholly cooled; the family were all forbidden to weep. At ten in the morning, a fellow practitioner tested: everywhere else was cold, but the crown alone remained hot as boiling water.

Like Layman Lien Hang — precepts incomplete, practice still shallow — yet attaining rebirth, it is clear, wholly through fellow practitioners' supporting recitation. See here how essential Buddha-recitation support is for practitioners at life's end. This was the eighteenth day of the seventh month in the thirteenth year of the Republic; the layman was but thirty years old.

THE POOR WOMAN

In Cixi county, Zhejiang, there was a poor woman whose name is not clearly known. Her house was utterly poor, and her son, moreover, was unfilial, often scolding and reviling her.

One day, after her son's shouting, unable to bear the misery, she went to pour out her weeping to the monk of a nearby temple. The monk said: "Since you know suffering, why not sell it!". The poor woman answered: "Who would buy suffering — how could it be sold!". The monk said: "If with utmost sincerity you devotedly recite Amitabha's sacred name, seeking the West, then at life's end the Buddha will surely escort you to the Land of Bliss. Thenceforth forever parted from all sufferings, purely enjoying every joy. That is called selling suffering!". She replied: "Mother and son share one thatched hut; the house corner is the kitchen, and beneath the bed is even a pigsty. Amid such mixed filth, how recite the Buddha's name?". The monk answered: "No hindrance. The Thus Come One, greatly kind and compassionate, delivers according to conditions, using only a sincere mind, regardless of form. When free, come worship at the temple; at home simply
recite devotedly and vow rebirth — that suffices.". The poor woman followed his teaching; longing eagerly to escape suffering, she recited unbroken.

Three years later, some months before her passing, she told her son: "On such a day, Mother shall be born into the West. Do not go away; stay home and manage the funeral to fulfill a child's love!". The son showed disbelief, but she, foreseeing, repeated the charge several times. A few days before her passing, the son suddenly smelled a strange fragrance spreading boundlessly. Searching everywhere, he found no one burning incense, wondering whence the scent came. Suddenly remembering his mother's words, he thought it might be true, and stayed home to wait. On the appointed day, he saw his mother heat water, bathe, and change into clean robes. Done, she sat upon the bed facing west, recited awhile, and breathed her last. This was about the tenth year of the Republic.

Appended note: While at Buddha's Summit temple, I myself heard Dharma Master Dinh Tay recount this story. Regrettably, being busy then, I did not ask clearly the person's name nor the date of rebirth. Recorded by Monk Duc Sum.

A comment: Alas! The sea of dust spreads wide, karmic waves surging high. The borrowed realm is fragile, lives sinking in suffering! Consider the poor woman: her spiritual nature long present, yet through delusion she strayed into rebirth. Suffering's abyss lay deep, yet blessed conditions met the wondrous gate. Three years of earnest grief-moved diligence, and at the end strange fragrance spread. Toward the West one sincere earnest heart; casting off the illusory substance, she ascended the jeweled tower. Thus know Amitabha's great vows: the deliverance boat refuses no one. And the Pure Land's wondrous Dharma: the excelling lotus grade is gained even in this life. The golden pond's blossoms stand fair — awaiting only the resolute.

LITTLE VUONG

Little Vuong was the servant of Layman Ton Hau Tai, a native of Hubei. Being surnamed Vuong and of the lowly station of a servant boy, all called him Little Vuong. Master Ton's family were Buddhists; later moving to Shanghai, Little Vuong followed his master.

Serving long in Ton Hau Tai's house and influenced by its practice, Little Vuong gradually absorbed it and resolved to recite the Buddha's name. He had a stutter, speaking slowly and with difficulty, so at first his recitation formed no sentences. Yet with a sincere heart he strove on. After some years, one night Little Vuong dreamed someone seized his tongue and pulled it hard; startled, he felt great pain. Upon waking, the old defect was severed; speech flowed glibly, and his recitation came clear and distinct. His faith and vows grew profound, his recitation ever more diligent. After two more years, Little Vuong was no longer heard reciting. Asked why, he answered: "My recitation has formed a single mass; now without reciting it recites of itself, so no need to voice it — only rouse the mind to keep the sacred name silently continuous, unbroken.". Hearing this, none paid much mind, leaving him to his way. Such silent mindfulness continued another five springs and autumns.

One day Little Vuong called home his fifteen-year-old son, then lodging at school, saying: "Today Father is leaving; help with recitation to see me off one last time!". All saw him still normal, showing not the least illness, and disbelieved, only smiling at one another. Little Vuong, reading their minds, said: "I speak truly, no idle joke!". Asked: "Leaving — but where?". Answer: "Going home!". Pressed: "Which home, where?". Answer: "The old homeland — the Western Land of Bliss!". One present, moved to faith, said: "Your son is still small; stay a few more years to shelter him till he can stand alone!". Little Vuong said: "The season has come; I cannot linger. All matters I entrust to conditions!". Having spoken, he had his son chant with him. All stood silent, watching to see what would unfold. Little Vuong sat upright upon the bed reciting. Then suddenly he sneezed twice, displaying the mark of jade secretions drooping down, closed his eyes, and departed directly. (That is, two streams of thick white mucus hanging down — a sign of one casting off the body in transcendent passing.)

The next evening, Layman Ton Hau Tai had the remains cremated at Luu Van Temple. When the fire was lit, the pyre blazed up, white smoke rising straight to the sky. Above the flames appeared a great radiant lotus flickering, ever clearer and fairer, until the fire died. At dawn, examining the ashes and bones, the form of a lotus stood out plainly as if drawn. This was the sixteenth year of the Republic.
