SOME REFLECTIONS ON THE 50 STORIES OF THE DELIVERANCE MISSION
(Quang Từ)
Having read all 50 stories of the Deliverance Mission (Sứ Mệnh Độ Vong), and looking at them through the eyes of a lay practitioner, what lingers is not fear, but a profound stillness deep in the heart. For the more one reads, the clearer becomes a truth that most people in this world avoid facing: no death is accidental, and no suffering is without cause. Everything is the fruition of causes once sown, simply waiting for the right conditions to ripen. The spirits in these stories did not end up as hungry and thirsty ghosts, or imprisoned in painful hells, for no reason. They once lived as we live now — they had families, thoughts, and choices. The difference is that while alive they let themselves drift, taking good and evil karma lightly, so that when they died, all that remained was the karmic force pulling them into the realm matching their deeds.

Looking back over each story, the workings of cause and effect stand out unmistakably. Some stole out of greed and harmed others, and after death had to endure ghastly punishments, starving and thirsting to the point of not daring to eat even when food was offered. Some made their living by killing other beings, and in the end those very lives returned to torment them — tearing, flaying, and skinning them, repaying every shred of pain they had inflicted. Some were unfilial, cursing their own parents, and in hell were tortured in its deepest levels with pain beyond words. None of this is senseless punishment; it is the mirror image of their own actions and intentions while alive. A seed of karma may be sown small, but its fruit can drag on for years, even for many lifetimes.

That is why, while still alive, the most important thing for a human being is not lofty pursuits like great wealth, fame, or renown — it is holding firm to the basic moral foundation of a human being:
1. Upholding the Five Precepts: not killing, so as to avoid sowing enmity with other beings; not stealing, so as to create no debts of wrongdoing; no sexual misconduct (no adultery, no wrecking the happiness of others, no masturbation), so as to keep body and mind pure; no lying or harsh speech, so as to create no unwholesome karma of the mouth; and no intoxicants, so as to preserve mindfulness. It sounds simple, yet looking back over these stories, nearly every spirit violated these very rules. It was precisely because they could not keep the Five Precepts that they opened the door to evil karma themselves, and when the fruit ripened there was no escape.

It sounds simple, yet looking back over the 50 stories, nearly every spirit violated these very rules:
Pig butchers → killing → butchered in return like pigs
Thieves → violent death, hell
Those guilty of sexual misconduct → gruesome death, heavy karma
Those guilty of harsh speech → tongues ripped out, torture
Drunkards → ruin for themselves and others
The Buddha taught in the Consecration Sutra (Quán Đỉnh Kinh): "For every precept a Buddhist disciple upholds, five guardian gods watch over and protect them. Thus one who fully keeps the Five Precepts (the 5 prohibitions) is constantly attended and supported by twenty-five guardian gods, who dispel evil deeds and calamity." To keep the Five Precepts, then, is to raise an "invisible shield" around oneself.

But merely "doing no evil" is not enough. A practitioner must go one step further: practicing
2. The Ten Wholesome Deeds. That is, not merely avoiding evil, but actively doing good through body, speech, and mind. When the body does no killing and holds compassion for all beings; when the mouth speaks no evil and knows kind, wholesome words; when the mind is free of greed and anger and gives rise to right mindfulness — then merit naturally grows. When merit is sufficient, guardian gods protect you, and unseen evil forces can hardly harm you. One who lives in the light of a wholesome heart is not easily followed by spirits, not easily harassed by demons, and not easily struck by sudden accidents. Conversely, a heart full of greed, anger, and delusion emits, as it were, a matching frequency, drawing corresponding realms to itself, so that life is never at peace.

One thing well worth pondering is that many deaths in these stories look senseless: beaten to death, sudden accidents, or deaths in terribly unjust circumstances. Yet through the lens of cause and effect, these are not "bad luck" but karma come to fruition. Some once caused others suffering, and in this life must suffer it back; some once created enmity, and now have their lives claimed; some once held life cheap, and so their own life is taken in the blink of an eye. Cause and effect plays no favorites and overlooks no one. It is only that while the fruit has yet to come, people delude themselves into thinking they are "fine."

How, then, can we protect ourselves from falling into the painful realms those spirits endure? First of all, keep the heart upright and live a wholesome life. In addition, a lay practitioner can recite mantras daily, such as the Great Compassion Mantra, the Shurangama Mantra, or the Six-Syllable Great Bright Mantra. This is not superstition but a method for settling the mind and raising inner energy, thereby creating a natural "protective field." Moreover, when one recites the Buddhas' mantras while strictly keeping the Five Precepts, the Dharma protectors guard one's body and mind, sparing the practitioner sudden accidents.

Furthermore, venerating and taking refuge in Bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin), Ksitigarbha, or the Medicine Buddha is also an excellent practice. Not to beg favors blindly, but to learn from their compassionate vows to relieve suffering. As we make offerings in worship, our own heart gradually grows to resemble the Bodhisattvas' heart, and life naturally transforms for the better.

To take one example, in Chapter 11 (The Earth Spirit as Dharma Protector) of the Sutra of Ksitigarbha's Fundamental Vows, Kiên Lao, the Earth Spirit, addressed the Buddha, saying that wherever people use earth, stone, bamboo, or wood to build a chapel or hut, or use gold, silver, copper, or iron to cast an image of Ksitigarbha Bodhisattva and then burn incense, make offerings, and pay homage, that place will enjoy ten benefits:
1. Fertile land: the soil there becomes rich and productive.
2. A safe and stable home: the household stays forever peaceful and secure.
3. The departed are born in heaven: ancestors and deceased family members ascend to heavenly realms.
4. The living enjoy blessings: those still living in the house receive many benefits and blessings.
5. Every prayer answered: all rightful wishes come true.
6. No calamity of water or fire: no floods or conflagrations.
7. All loss eliminated: no loss of property, no ruinous business, no shortfall.
8. Nightmares ended for good: the family is no longer plagued by bad dreams and sleeps soundly.
9. Guardian spirits coming and going: benevolent gods escort and protect you whenever you leave or return.
10. Meetings with holy beings: frequent encounters with wise teachers and saints who instruct you.

In particular, one thing that must not be overlooked is repentance toward our karmic creditors. Much suffering in life comes not from outside but from unresolved enmities of the past. When we turn back to repent, dedicate merit, and apologize from the bottom of our hearts, those knots of resentment gradually loosen and life grows lighter (this is explained further below, in the section on repenting to karmic creditors).

Alongside this, doing good — giving alms, helping others, making offerings to the Triple Gem, not wasting food, and the like — is a swift way to accumulate merit. Merit is the sturdiest "shield" protecting us from unseen calamities. The meritorious turn even disaster aside lightly, while for those lacking merit, a small matter can become a great calamity.

Finally, one sincere message: the spirits in these stories are not strangers. They once lived, thought, and erred just as we do today. With one difference — they have exhausted their chance to make amends. We, on the other hand — still breathing, still with a tomorrow — still have ours. Do not wait until this human body is lost to understand the Dharma, for then, however deep the regret, there is no turning back. While alive, keep the Five Precepts, practice the Ten Wholesome Deeds, repent, and nurture compassion. Do this, and without praying for anything lofty, guardian gods will naturally protect you, life will be peaceful, and the road ahead will brighten with every wholesome step you take.
