WHY SHOULD WE ASPIRE FOR REBIRTH IN THE WESTERN PURE LAND IN THIS LIFE, RATHER THAN REMAINING IN THE SAHA WORLD TO TRANSMIGRATE, OR SEEKING BIRTH IN HEAVEN?
The Western Pure Land of Ultimate Bliss of Amitabha Buddha, as described in sutras such as the Amitabha Sutra, the Sutra of Immeasurable Life, and the Contemplation Sutra, is a pure, majestic, blissful realm beyond every suffering of the Saha world. There, there is no birth, aging, sickness, or death; no enmity; no strife or rivalry; all beings live in the light of wisdom and compassion. The ground is formed of seven treasures; the lakes are crystal clear with waters of eight virtues; birds sing the Dharma; even the wind becomes a reminder to practice. All who are born in the Pure Land bear majestic forms, minds free of affliction, dwelling daily near Buddhas and Bodhisattvas, hearing the Dharma and practicing without backsliding until the fruit of the path is won.

Countless eons ago, Amitabha Buddha vowed to receive all beings: when approaching death, whoever holds true faith, earnest aspiration, and recites His name ten times will be escorted to that land. This is a broad road open to everyone, of high or low capacity alike — one need only turn back toward goodness.

From beginningless time we have drifted through birth and death for countless lives. In some lives we once aroused the bodhi mind heroically, resolved to practice diligently and escape birth and death for good — yet after a while, from that first life into later ones, stained by the world we lost the bodhi mind, until we committed every evil: the realms of gods, spirits, humans, hungry ghosts, animals, and hells we have all passed through, only we do not remember — carried on by karma through continuous birth and death. And so through countless lives, numberless as the sands of the Ganges, we still have not escaped birth and death. The truth is, if we think of staying in this world to practice until attaining Arhatship (the saintly fruit that ends transmigration), or even the first fruit of Stream-Entry (the first of the Four Stages of Sainthood, guaranteeing Arhatship to come) — it is immensely, immensely hard. One might say: practicing on in this world for a million lives, there is no certainty of attainment.

Why is that?
Because our realm is a defiled evil age: in this life we may practice, eat vegetarian, and do many meritorious deeds, only to be born next life into a rich house, forget our original bodhi mind, and merrily indulge the five desires — for great blessings easily breed evil karma — and at death fall back into hell; then perhaps practice good again in a following life, until a single thought of pride, lust, or anger at the deathbed moment drags us down... and so it goes: good karma reaps blessings, evil karma reaps torment... attainment all but impossible. Even birth in heavens like the Trayastrimsha heaven of blissful merit (what some religions call paradise), with lifespans of millions of earthly years — when merit runs out, one falls as before (into hell, hungry ghosts, or animals, according to karma). Today we have met the Buddha-recitation Dharma door: whoever holds deep, firm faith in the Pure Land and in Amitabha Buddha, practices bravely, eats vegetarian, keeps the precepts, and vows to recite the Buddha's name seeking rebirth in the Western Pure Land, may be reborn there. Reborn at the Middle Grade, Middle Birth, after seven days the lotus opens, and hearing Amitabha Buddha preach the Dharma, one at once attains the first fruit of Stream-Entry; after half an eon — some eight million years — Arhatship, then onward practice until Buddhahood; or at the Middle Grade, Upper Birth, as the lotus just cracks open one hears voices praising the Four Noble Truths and instantly attains Arhatship (according to the Sutra of Immeasurable Life), then practices onward to Buddhahood.

We see that if we strive in this life, after rebirth there our liberation is assured — even at the lowest grade of the lowest birth, never again to suffer transmigration. This Dharma door allows rebirth carrying one's karma along — most favorable for beings of today's Dharma-ending age: one need only, at life's end, recite Amitabha Buddha's name ten times and vow rebirth in that land, and Amitabha Buddha will come to escort you. Though you may still be an ordinary mortal full of base thoughts, if at the deathbed moment you diligently recite the Buddha's name and let go of all attachments, you will surely be reborn there — by His eighteenth great vow, borrowing His empowering grace for your rebirth.

Consider: this, or drifting hundreds of millions of lives in transmigration — which is easier? Knowing the Buddha-recitation Dharma door in this life, we must never neglect it!

CLOSING WORDS
And so closes the journey of the DELIVERANCE MISSION (Sứ Mệnh Độ Vong): fifty stories — fifty different fates, fifty lives adrift in the current of cause and effect, and above all fifty quiet lessons for each of us. Each story is not merely a glimpse of the underworld or of the mysteries beyond death, but a clear reflection of human life in this world: greed, anger, and delusion; love and hatred; good and evil endlessly intertwined. I wonder, reading these final lines, how you feel — a shiver, a pang, or a silence, glimpsing somewhere the shadow of yourself in each fate retold.

The DELIVERANCE MISSION does not end here. That journey has only closed one door to open a new road — deeper, more worldly, and closer to each of us. The next part, titled THE CROSSROADS OF THE LIVING AND THE DEAD (Giao Lộ Âm Dương), will be a different leg of the road, where the boundary between the living and the departed fades, where the stories are no longer so strange or purely frightening, but real, worldly, painful, and deeply human. There, each character may be a friend, a relative — or even ourselves in some errant moment of life.

Throughout every part, the stories' shared aim is not to sow fear, nor to glorify hell or the underworld. What matters most is reminding people to live more wholesomely, to look back at themselves, correct themselves, and strive to do useful things for life and for others. For hell is not something that merely waits for us after we close our eyes. Hell sometimes already exists while we live — in the pangs of conscience, in sleepless nights over past wrongs, in the emptiness of having lost the goodness we once had.

If we stop in time, keep the precepts, discipline the body, tame the karma of speech, and purify the mind, then this present life can itself become a peaceful realm. Heaven or hell, in the end, both begin with how we think, speak, and act each day. One cruel word can push another into suffering, but one kind word can save a despairing soul. One wrong act can drag consequences through a lifetime, but one turning back can open a new road.

If, reading these stories, some do not yet believe, please take this as a spiritual book for reflection — read to ponder, to listen, to feel. And if anyone finds something useful in it, some detail that touches the heart, take your own lessons from it. If but one person lives better after reading, one person stops before a wrongdoing, this journey will not have been in vain.

In compiling this work, shortcomings and limits in content and expression were surely unavoidable. I humbly ask readers for tolerance, generosity, and understanding. The road ahead is still long, and I hope that in the coming CROSSROADS OF THE LIVING AND THE DEAD (Giao Lộ Âm Dương), we will meet again — not in fear, but in wakefulness and goodwill.

COMMENTS:
After reading the story of VIRGINITY TRADING, I sat quietly for a long time. Not because of fear, but because we see one thing clearly: in each of us there are times of weakness, times when due to circumstances, pressure, or for some reason we have chosen paths that we can't help but feel sad when looking back on.This story reminds me that Karma is not something far away or only exists after death. It is present in the small, everyday choices. A word, an action, a decision for "love" or for "money" - all are sowing seeds. And sooner or later, we will be the ones reaping the harvest.

But what warms my heart after every story like this is: it's never too late. Even though they have caused heavy karma, even though they have endured suffering for many years and many lifetimes, the spirits in the book still have the opportunity to let go, have the opportunity to be guided, repented and transformed. That is the power of mindfulness and compassion.

For those of you reading these lines, perhaps you are not someone who has caused great karma like the character in the story. But each of us has times when we make mistakes, times when we hurt others because of selfishness or ignorance. This story is a gentle reminder: stop while you still can. Let's choose a way to live so that when night comes, we can sleep peacefully with a peaceful conscience.

An act of kindness, a sincere apology, a decision to live a good life today - no matter how small - can change the fate of ourselves and those around us for many lifetimes to come. That's what I want to convey after listening to this story.
