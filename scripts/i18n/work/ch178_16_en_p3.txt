{align:justify|Professor Truong had three sons, each outstanding—above all the eldest: graduating college he went to America to study, then married and built a career. Two years ago, Professor Truong traveled from America to visit her second and youngest sons in Taiwan, when suddenly a call reported the eldest choking while bathing, now in emergency care. By the time she reached America, the eldest had died.}
{align:justify|To her it was a bolt from the blue; she collapsed, fainting. Through her mother's counsel she forced back the pain to complete his forty-nine days. By now she wept night and day—no comfort availed.}
{align:justify|Her youngest son came to Los Angeles, even leaving work to soothe his mother's grief. Professor Truong too hoped to climb out of the pain—so she thought of visiting a temple never once entered, hoping the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas might steady her mind.}
{align:justify|Ten years earlier Professor Truong had known of this temple, yet never visited. That day she went with her husband and youngest son; entering, she saw two people in the Buddha hall: one a Hong Kong Dharma master, one a lay resident. The Hong Kong monastic, seeing Mrs. Truong, stared fixedly—while Professor Truong then wept at the sight of anyone. The master asked why; learning the matter, he kept telling her:}
{align:justify|"Rest easy—I shall help you."}
{align:justify|Though first meeting, the master asked her telephone number and set tomorrow to meet. Thereafter weekly he met and talked with her, often saying: "The world is impermanent, ever parting in birth and death…" aiming to console and wake her—but she could never climb out of misery's abyss.}
{align:justify|Truly, all her life Professor Truong had done nothing to shame her conscience; in Taiwan she devoted herself to social charity, aiding countless youths and adults to study well, opening their wisdom. Why should this befall her?}
{align:justify|One day the master telephoned, inviting her over, saying:}
{align:justify|"I dearly wish to help you, but the cure must fit the disease. Yet I cannot trace your hidden cause and effect, so I cannot help. I have invited an Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) statue from the Mainland, now kept in my Buddha hall—are you fond of Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) Bodhisattva?"}
{align:justify|"Since childhood I have deeply admired Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) Bodhisattva!"}
{align:justify|The master said:}
{align:justify|"Arrange thus: to invite Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) Bodhisattva home, first buy sixty-six centimeters of yellow cloth, two bunches of chrysanthemums, with fruit, candles, incense… then tomorrow morning come to invite Her…"}
{align:justify|That day she went with her son to choose cloth. Yellow cloth abounded; wavering, not knowing which to buy, suddenly she saw a most unusual bolt—picked it up, ready to pay. The shopkeeper said:}
{align:justify|"This piece is not for sale—it is too short. Bought home, it could serve nothing." Measuring, Professor Truong found exactly sixty-six centimeters—so she bought it at once. Passing the flower stalls, exactly two bunches of chrysanthemums remained—she bought them too, and next day invited the Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) statue home.}
{align:justify|From then, for more than two months, Professor Truong still felt great misery, washing her face daily with tears. Once at the master's place, he said:}
{align:justify|"I must trace your cause and effect clearly—your case differs from ordinary people's; I must use special means to help you."}
{align:justify|Then he handed her a red paper and an envelope, saying:}
{align:justify|"On this paper write: metal, wood, water, fire, earth."}
{align:justify|Seeing her bewildered, he explained:}
{align:justify|"Metal: write of family background, birth origins. Wood: write of friends and work. Water: write the names of your whole family. Fire: what injustices have you in this life? Earth: what wishes have you? But heed one point: absolutely let no one see it—including husband, children, even myself."}
{align:justify|Written, then on that day, hour, month, year… facing that direction… read it five times, three days running, fifteen readings in all. Done, burn it, place the ashes in this envelope beneath your pillow.}
{align:justify|Professor Truong did exactly as told, writing more than sixteen hundred characters, reading fifteen rounds complete, burning it, placing it in the envelope beneath her pillow.}
{align:justify|A week passed; the master telephoned, asking:}
{align:justify|"In this time has any phenomenon occurred?"}
{align:justify|Professor Truong answered:}
{align:justify|"No."}
{align:justify|On the tenth day, the master called to say:}
{align:justify|"Congratulations—I have traced your cause and effect clearly; come quickly to my place."}
{align:justify|Her youngest son accompanied her.}
{align:justify|At the master's, she saw a very large yellow paper on his desk, densely covered with writing.}
{align:justify|The master said:}
{align:justify|"Last night I sat in meditation till dawn, at last tracing everything from the water altar. Meaning: whatever characters appeared in the water, I copied out—hours to finish copying—all recorded on this paper. Read it and you shall understand at once."}
{align:justify|Then the master told her:}
{align:justify|"Two more names for you to see." She glanced and said:}
{align:justify|"These two I surely know—they have come to collect their debts from me."}
{align:justify|The master said:}
{align:justify|"Do you hate these two deeply?"}
{align:justify|"Yes."}
{align:justify|The master said:}
{align:justify|"You should not hate them—in a former life you owed them; hate no more. Everything bears its own cause and effect."}
{align:justify|"Next the master handed her the yellow paper, saying:}
{align:justify|Reading the first passage, your heart shall fill with fear; reading the second, you shall marvel greatly; reading the third, you shall feel boundless comfort.}
{align:justify|So she began reading the document—very long: the whole large page detailing her former life. Astonishingly, this story of karmic retribution matched what the Taichung master told nineteen years before—only here recorded more fully, with names and surnames complete:}
{align:justify|It is history's famed "Dou E case," known to all—a wholly true story recorded thus:}
{align:justify|"Professor Truong in a former life was Prefect Tao Wu, a corrupt official, wicked in every way: he threw Dou E into prison, wrongly sentencing her to death. Dou E thereby bound a blood-deep feud with the corrupt official, vowing lifetime after lifetime to hunt him for vengeance, bringing his clan ever to 'house wrecked, line destroyed' (reading this passage, she trembled with fear).}
{align:justify|Briefly, the "Dou E case":}
{align:justify|"Dou E, motherless from childhood, had a father—a poor scholar named Dou Tianzhang—who, wishing to sit the capital exams yet lacking travel money, borrowed twenty taels of silver from the widowed mother-in-law surnamed Cai.}
{align:justify|Unexpectedly, the next year the debt (at high interest) became forty taels; Mr. Zhang unable to repay, had to give his daughter Dou E both as debt payment and as a child-bride raised by the Cai household.}
{align:justify|Ten years in the Cai house, Dou E was wed into the Cai line. But within two years, her husband died. Widowed, Dou E stayed to keep her mother-in-law company.}
{align:justify|At the time, in Chuzhou lived Zhang Lu'er, a ruffian: conspiring with old Zhang his father, together they bullied the two widowed Cais, forcing Mrs. Cai to marry old Zhang. To save her life, the mother-in-law had no choice but to accept old Zhang. But Zhang Lu'er's ambition stopped not there—he pressed Dou E to marry him. Dou E firmly refused, and Zhang Lu'er plotted to ruin her.}
{align:justify|One day her mother-in-law fell ill, and Dou E cooked a nourishing soup to serve her. But Zhang Lu'er secretly poisoned the soup, scheming first to kill Mrs. Cai, then force Dou E to wed him.}
{align:justify|Unexpectedly, Mrs. Cai felt nauseous and did not take the soup; old Zhang, seeing fine fare, seized and finished it. Moments later the poison struck—he fell and died.}
{align:justify|Events fell wholly outside his plan: meaning to kill his stepmother, Zhang Lu'er killed his own father. Deceitfully, he pinned the murder on Dou E and sued before the officials.}
{align:justify|Chuzhou's prefect was corrupt; bribed heavily by Zhang Lu, he tortured Dou E to confess. But Dou E would rather be beaten to death than confess—so the prefect turned to torturing and beating Mrs. Cai. Pitying her mother-in-law, to save her from the lash and torture, Dou E swallowed tears and confessed.}
{align:justify|Thus sentenced to death, she was bound and led to the execution ground. Then Dou E, heart brimming with grief and rage, pointed at heaven and earth, crying:}
{align:justify|"Heaven—letting the worthy suffer oppression and wrongful judgment—and you call yourself heaven? Earth—knowing neither good from bad, straight from crooked—and you call yourself earth?"}
{align:justify|As the headsman raised his great blade, lightning flashing, Dou E looked up at heaven and loudly read three vows, swearing:}
{align:justify|"If my sentence is unjust, then when the blade passes my neck, my blood shall not fall but fly straight to the white banner—not one drop of fresh blood staining my corpse upon the ground."}
{align:justify|"Throughout the sixth month (high summer), heaven must send snow three feet deep covering my corpse."}
{align:justify|"Dead, all Chuzhou prefecture must suffer three straight years of drought, proving my wrongful death."}
{align:justify|Her vows sworn, the headsman swung—the blood indeed flew straight to the white banner; next, all June heaven sent great snow covering her corpse. And after her death, Chuzhou suffered three years' drought.}
{align:justify|Dou E's wishes were fulfilled, proving plainly her wrongful death. This sounded an earth-shaking summons: all Chuzhou's people hated the corrupt Tao Wu utterly, sympathizing deeply with young Dou E wrongfully slain"…}
{align:justify|This historical tale later inspired many scripts, one titled "Snow in June"—applauded through a hundred thousand years; its author "Quan Hán Khanh" grew famed, regarded as a master, a world-class playwright.}
{align:justify|But in the performed script a conclusion was added:}
{align:justify|"After Dou E's father Dou Tianzhang passed the exams to high office, he came by imperial order to Chuzhou to survey the people's plight, finding the region drought-stricken, three years without a drop of rain. Thinking surely an injustice lay behind it, all that night he kept lamplight re-examining Dou E's case. Suddenly light flashed—Dou E appeared, telling her injustice in full, begging her father to uphold justice.}
{align:justify|Dou Tianzhang investigated the case minutely, retried it, declared Dou E innocent, sentenced Zhang Lu'er to death, executed at Chuzhou. The sentence pronounced, heaven rained in torrents"…}
{align:justify|Reading the second passage, Mrs. Zhang marveled indeed—for on that day… she had written this "Heavenly Memorial" (exactly as the master taught, then burned)—revealing it to none—yet the master had received its whole contents and copied them out (exactly the 1633 characters she wrote), like a photocopy.}
{align:justify|Reading the third passage, the master had traced and concluded: "In this very life, Professor Truong has never done anything evil. In Taiwan she aided countless young men and women to study well, opening their wisdom. She is a filial daughter, serving her mother wholeheartedly, daring disobey her in nothing"…}
{align:justify|So the master advised her now to strive in repentance. He said:}
{align:justify|"You should sincerely repent, diligently learn Buddhism, cultivate vigorously."}
{align:justify|Reading the final passage, she felt extraordinary comfort—bowing to the master, thanking him for all he had done for her.}
{align:justify|The master hastily said:}
{align:justify|"No need to thank me—rightly I must thank you. Do you know why I came here? For had I not traced your cause and effect, I might never return to Hong Kong!"}
{align:justify|Seeing Mrs. Zhang bewildered, the monastic explained::}
{align:justify|"My master in Hong Kong has entered retreat—rightly 'asking nothing of worldly affairs,' no longer minding the world. But long ago he received your kindness (once you saved him), and in retreat he discovered you in calamity, greatly suffering… So he sent me to bring this Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) statue to Los Angeles and find you—failing, never dream of returning to Hong Kong…"}
{align:justify|My first day here, I knew roughly your age (though you lived nearby)—but Los Angeles is so vast, where could I search?… so I was certain return looked hopeless.}
{align:justify|I entered this temple, asking hopefully whether any woman like you… happened by—never dreaming you would appear the next day. The moment I met you I knew: this is the one I seek, the very one my master sent me to meet… But then I could not say so plainly, only eagerly promising to help…}
{align:justify|Now your cause and effect is traced—I shall carry these papers to Hong Kong, for my master wishes to help you; in Hong Kong now a Platform is raised, a most solemn, grand 'Ten Thousand Buddhas Repentance' assembly.}
{align:justify|For this is a former-life life-debt, an age-old injustice most hard to deliver—only an immense assembly may hope to dissolve it.}
{align:justify|Now you need worry no more; hereafter you shall have the chance to visit Hong Kong and bow to my master. What you must do now is repent daily.}
{align:justify|Professor Truong, in that moment moved, feeling her sins so deep and heavy, at once quit her work, vowing to spend her remaining days single-mindedly in repentance"…}
{align:justify|Finishing her story, Professor Truong concluded:}
{align:justify|"None of us must pass through such uncanny events; but from others' stories we too may draw fine lessons!}
{align:justify|I only regret repenting too late—if possible, I hope you all first hasten to repent, and whatever karma exists shall surely dissolve."}
{align:justify|Learning Professor Truong's story, this writer was profoundly moved and spared no ink recording it fully, hoping the destined may read it and wake, reforming body and mind…}
{align:justify|I take full responsibility for this article's truthfulness; Professor Truong now lives in Los Angeles, and my senior in America has checked and confirmed it entirely true.}
