TITLE: 21. THE BEAUTY AND THE BANDIT
TOMTAT: The beautiful Miss Trieu was violated by her stepfather, lost her murdered child, was robbed of her goods, and assaulted by thugs — because in a past life she was a bandit who robbed and violated women. Meeting Master Miaofa, she learned the cause and effect and how to transform it.
-----
{align:center|**THE BEAUTY AND THE BANDIT**}

{align:justify|A woman settled overseas came seeking Master Miaofa. She introduced herself:}

{align:justify|"Venerable, I am a lawyer. I have just taken a most peculiar divorce case: a young married woman named Trieu — lovely, dignified, yet utterly haggard in spirit. She begs us to help her divorce her husband, on the grounds that he is brutally violent."}

{align:justify|We accepted the case and arranged to meet Mr. Ly, her husband. On meeting him we were taken aback: her husband's face is most kind and blessed. He is a refined man of bearing, nothing coarse or fierce as his wife described. After we told him his wife insisted on divorce, his face filled with pity and worry for her. He kept pleading, begging us to help persuade his wife for him. A thousand times, ten thousand times, divorce is impossible. For his wife's body and spirit are continually suffering ruin at the hands of wicked, inhuman villains. Hence his wife's spirit is distraught, hallucinating. Divorced, he fears his wife could hardly live, with no husband beside her to care for her..."}

{align:justify|Then he recounted his wife's whole life, telling clearly from childhood what misfortunes she had met. Honestly, hearing of her tragic fate, our whole legal team was shaken, astonished without end. We deeply sympathized with the family's ill fortune and wished with all our strength to help them. But however we strove to mediate, the wife kept insisting: "Without divorce, no peace!"}

{align:justify|In this impasse we were helpless. So seizing my leave, I have come here to ask you: why does that beautiful young woman bear so wayward and cruel a fate? Can she change her unlucky destiny? Can her spirit fully recover? We beg the Master's teaching.}

{align:justify|Then the woman lawyer briefly told the Master what the couple had endured.}

{align:justify|Mr. Ly and Miss Trieu came from Hong Kong and now live in Canada. Childhood sweethearts from primary through high school, as the years grew so their love deepened. Mr. Ly gentle and refined; Miss Trieu beautiful and dignified — truly a heaven-made pair.}

{align:justify|After high school Mr. Ly proposed to Miss Trieu — to his shock she wept bitterly, saying she was unworthy to marry him. Pressed long, Mr. Ly at last learned the truth.}

{align:justify|When Miss Trieu was eight her father died; her mother remarried. At 14 Miss Trieu was raped by her brutal stepfather; though her mother knew, she could only grit her teeth and abandon her stepdaughter to the stepfather's prolonged violation for years on end, until this very day. Hearing it, Mr. Ly burned with hatred and deep compassion, and resolved to rescue his beloved from the demon's den.}

{align:justify|Mr. Ly's father traded in shoes; the family was most rich. Mr. Ly hid Miss Trieu's violation by her stepfather, confessed his love to his parents, and asked to bring Miss Trieu to Canada to continue university together, marrying after graduation.}

{align:justify|His parents loved their son and cherished the pair's deep love, and granted their wish. Graduated, they married and opened a shoe shop of their own. Before long a lovely boy was born. While the whole house lived happily, heaven suddenly raised a storm. Their son, just eight, was kidnapped; they paid hundreds of thousands in ransom, then found him murdered, his body abandoned in the wilds.}

{align:justify|Their darling's death shook the couple to the uttermost depths of pain — above all Miss Trieu, whose grief never eased over the long years, her spirit hard to restore. But calamities never come singly: the next year, a whole container truck of their goods was robbed clean, plunging them into grave economic straits. Yet ill fortune had not ended.}

{align:justify|One evening of the third year, as they prepared to close the shop, suddenly a great truck stopped before the gate; six fierce bandits leapt down and stormed inside, saying nothing — gagging and binding husband and wife. Then they loaded all the plundered goods onto the truck, stripped them of money and jewelry. Most enraging of all: they took turns raping the wife before her husband's eyes. From that day Miss Trieu's spirit utterly collapsed; ever distraught, often hallucinating, she fixed upon her husband as a villain who battered, raped, and beat her — and firmly demanded divorce.}

{align:justify|The woman lawyer finished and asked the Master:}

{align:justify|"What karma did Miss Trieu create in a past life to suffer so bitter a fate in this one? We beg the Master to tell us."}

{align:justify|All seated there were shaken hearing this wretched tale. Unimaginable that so frail a woman should receive such continuous wounds to spirit and body. Hard not to blame an unjust heaven!}

{align:justify|Then the Master sighed and said:}

{align:justify|"That young woman in a past life was a man named X, his family destitute, parents dead young; since childhood he had to beg, then herd goats for a landlord. Unable at last to bear inhuman abuse, X fled, drifting everywhere, living by theft. Grown, X became a bandit — beating men, robbing homes, seizing women to rape. There was no evil he did not do."}

{align:justify|Once, raiding a village, X saw a beautiful woman; X bound her husband and raped the wife before her husband's eyes. For years after, X kept returning to bully and degrade them; the victim couple, terrified of X's ferocity, could only grit their teeth and live in humiliation.}

{align:justify|In this life, the bandit X is reborn as the young woman Trieu; the brutal stepfather who violated her for years on end is in a past life that very beautiful woman once degraded by X — now reborn, seeking her enemy for revenge.}

{align:justify|As for Miss Trieu's murdered child, her robbed goods, her violation by thugs in turn — all are retribution for her past-life crimes. Hence the saying: 'Would you know the cause of a past life — look at the fruit reaped in this one. Would you know the fruit of a future life — look at the cause sown in this one!' For heaven's net, though vast and wide, lets not a single hair slip through!"}

{align:justify|Though an evildoer in this life may escape the law's punishment, he cannot escape the law of cause and effect — he must repay the evil karma he created. The Buddhist sutras ever say: 'Karmic retribution follows like shadow after form; though a hundred lives and a thousand ages pass, what was created is never lost'...}

{align:justify|This warns us to take utmost care: 'Do no evil; diligently do all good.'}

{align:justify|Hearing Master Miaofa, none disbelieved. All praised unceasingly the unfailing karmic retribution.}

{align:justify|Then someone wondered and asked the Master:}

{align:justify|"Miss Trieu in a past life as a bandit created teeming crimes — why in this life could she meet so devoted and righteous a husband?"}

{align:justify|All present joined in, gazing up awaiting the Master's answer.}

{align:justify|The Master slowly told:}

{align:justify|"One night, returning to the mountain stronghold after a raid, X met a boy in ragged clothes, wounded all over, weeping bitterly. Seeing this, X's heart softened, recalling his own childhood misery — and he pitied and understood. Asking, X learned the boy herded buffalo for a landlord; for losing a buffalo he had been savagely beaten and threatened: without compensation the landlord would report to the magistrate to clap his father in the stocks and prison!"}

{align:justify|Hearing it, the bandit felt compassion and gave the youth a large sum, bidding him use part to repay the buffalo's price, the remainder for his parents as capital for trade and livelihood.}

{align:justify|Receiving the money, the boy knocked his head to the bandit in thanks, moved to tears, vowing:}

{align:justify|"In the next life may I serve as ox and horse to repay life-saving grace..."}

{align:justify|That youth is now Miss Trieu's husband in this life. For this reason, whatever calamity Miss Trieu meets — even demanding divorce — Mr. Ly remains faithful from first to last, never parting, never wearying, ever loving and shielding her.}

{align:justify|The Master's gentle explanations were like the sun scattering the clouds of doubt in everyone's hearts.}

{align:justify|The Master continued:}

{align:justify|"People ordinarily know not the Buddha Dharma, understand not the law of retributive cause and effect following like shadow after form, and so deludedly create offenses, the scene arising with the mind. If Miss Trieu could receive the Buddha Dharma, all afflictions would melt like ice beneath sunlight — resolved at the root, severing the source of calamity and misfortune."}

{align:justify|If Miss Trieu could come to a pagoda, study the Buddha Dharma, keep the Five Precepts, practice the Ten Good Deeds, and each day reverently recite one copy of the Kshitigarbha Sutra dedicated to the beings she harmed in former lives, persevering diligently, her old karma shall dissolve. Afterward her destiny may turn for the better.}

{align:justify|The woman lawyer earnestly recorded the Master's teaching in her notebook; all breathed relief. They trusted that the pitiable Miss Trieu would soon escape her tragic fate through her own self-correction.}

{align:justify|COMMENTARY:}

{align:justify|The story THE BEAUTY AND THE BANDIT is among the most tragic and dramatic tales of the law of cause and effect. The beautiful Miss Trieu — violated for years by her stepfather, her child murdered, her goods robbed, gang-raped by thugs — all of it the fruit of a past life as a bandit robbing homes and raping women before their husbands' eyes. Yet because her husband was the "boy saved in a past life," however she demands divorce, he remains faithful in shelter. The story affirms: "Would you know the cause of a past life — look at the fruit reaped in this one." Though villains may escape earthly law, none escapes the law of cause and effect. The sole transformation is: "keep the Five Precepts, practice the Ten Good Deeds, and each day reverently recite one copy of the Kshitigarbha Sutra dedicated to the beings harmed in former lives." It is the strongest warning to all who still create evil karma: "Heaven's net is vast and wide; not a single hair slips through."}
