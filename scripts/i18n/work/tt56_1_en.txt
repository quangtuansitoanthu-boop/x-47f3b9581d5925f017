THE UPPER FASCICLE
{align:center|**THE MAHĀYĀNA SŪTRA ON THE MERIT OF MAKING BUDDHA IMAGES**}

{align:center|**THE UPPER FASCICLE**}

{align:center|**Under the Great Tang Dynasty: The Land of Khotan**}

{align:center|**Translated by Imperial Order by Tripiṭaka Śramaṇa Devaprajñā.**}

{align:center|**Rendered into Vietnamese by Bhikkhu Thích Thiện Thông of Vietnam.**}

{align:justify|Thus have I heard: At one time the Buddha was dwelling in the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, beneath the Pāriyātraka tree[1], together with an infinite assembly of great bhikkhus and infinite great Bodhisattvas, led by the Great Bodhisattva Maitreya.}

{align:justify|At that time, the World-Honored One kept the rains retreat of three months in that heaven, teaching the Dharma to his mother, bringing much benefit to the gods; countless gods left suffering behind and gained liberation. Countless gods were steeped in the teaching and obtained great blessed fruit. Among those gods was a young god whose lifespan was nearly exhausted, and the five signs of decline appeared[2]. Yet through the power of hearing the Dharma, after his death he was reborn again in this very heaven, leaving the evil paths behind for good.}

{align:justify|At that time, in Jambudvīpa there was no Buddha. It was as on a dark night full of stars but with no moon, as a land with no king, as a house with no master — all amusements and entertainments ceased entirely. Living beings then seemed orphaned, with no refuge to turn to. Toward the Buddha, everyone's heart bore a yearning love and deep sorrow, as at losing father and mother, as an arrow shot into the heart; together they went to where the Buddha had stayed, only to find groves, courtyards, and terraces silent and empty — everywhere vacant, with no Buddha — and their grief and longing only grew, unassuaged.}

{align:justify|At that time, King Udayana, dwelling in his palace, bore a constant sorrow, ever moved and yearning for the Buddha; the king cared nothing for his queens, his maidens, or his pleasures, and thought to himself: “In my present grief I shall not live long! How can I see the Buddha before I cast off this life?” Then he thought again: “When one loves someone dearly but cannot meet them, seeing the place where that person once stayed, or seeing someone exactly resembling them, may lessen the sorrow.” Pondering further, he reflected: “If I now go to the place where the Buddha once stayed[3] and do not see him, I shall surely weep in grief, moved to the point of death! I survey the world and find no one whose bodily marks, blessings, wisdom could equal the Tathāgata — how could I meet such a one to ease my sorrow?”}

{align:justify|Having aroused this thought, the king reflected further: “Now I shall commission the making of a Buddha image, to venerate and make offerings to.” But the king considered: “If the statue I make does not resemble the Buddha, I fear it will bring me measureless offense.” Then he thought again: “Suppose all the wise of the world[4] joined together to extol the Buddha's virtues, they still could not exhaust them. If any person praises him according to their share, that person still gains measureless blessing. So it is with me now: I should make a statue according to my own share.”}

{align:justify|The king at once commanded that all the skilled craftsmen of the land assemble. When all had gathered, the king told them:}

{align:justify|- Whoever can make an image of the Buddha for me, I shall repay and reward with jewels and treasures.}

{align:justify|The master craftsmen all memorialized the king:}

{align:justify|- What the king commands is truly a difficult task. The wondrous fine marks of the Tathāgata have no equal in this world. How could we fashion a Buddha image? Even if the god Viśvakarman[5] himself were to make it, he could never perfectly resemble the Tathāgata. If we accept the king's command to make a Buddha image, we might at best imitate in small part the curled hair and the white tuft; as for the other radiant, beautiful marks, full of august dignity — who could fashion them? The Buddha will soon return from heaven to the human world; should the statue turn out flawed, our good names would be ruined. We venture to say we dare not do it.}

{align:justify|King Udayana then replied:}

{align:justify|- My mind is resolved; do not refuse! As a man parched with thirst seeks living water to drink — would he refuse to drink only because he cannot drink it all?}

{align:justify|Hearing the king's words, the skilled craftsmen all knelt before him and memorialized:}

{align:justify|- We shall obey the command, but we beg the king to grant us this night to reflect; tomorrow morning we shall come to work.}

{align:justify|They further memorialized:}

{align:justify|- As the king makes this statue, it should be of pure sandalwood[6], whose grain is fine and whose substance is most durable. And should the Buddha's image be seated or standing, and of what height?}

{align:justify|The king put the question to his ministers. One well-informed minister came forth and memorialized:}

{align:justify|- The king should make the Tathāgata seated. Why? Because all Buddhas attain great awakening seated, turn the wheel of Dharma seated, display great miracles seated, subdue heterodox teachers seated, and perform great Buddha-deeds seated... all seated. Therefore the Buddha's image should be made seated in the cross-legged posture[7] upon a lion throne.}

{align:justify|At that very moment, the god Viśvakarman, seeing this affair from afar and discerning the king's wish to make a Buddha statue, pondered through the night: “My skill and understanding are most marvelous and rare; in all the world none equals me. If I make a statue for the king, it may somewhat resemble the Buddha[8].” He then transformed himself into a carpenter carrying sharp tools. Just as dawn broke, the man arrived to stand beside the palace gate, telling the gatekeeper to report clearly to the king: “I now wish to make a statue for the king; in craftsmanship none equals me. I humbly beg the king to appoint no other.”}

{align:justify|Hearing these words, the king was overjoyed and ordered the man invited in. Observing his bearing and conduct, the king knew him a master craftsman, and thought to himself: “How could such a man exist in the world? Could this be the god Viśvakarman or his disciple come hither?” Thereupon the king removed the jewel necklace he wore, and with his own hands draped it around Viśvakarman's neck, promising besides to reward his labor with all manner of jewels.}

{align:justify|The king at once went with the keeper of the storehouse into the treasury to select fragrant woods, and with his own shoulders bore them[9] forth to hand to the divine craftsman, telling him:}

{align:justify|- Excellent, Venerable Sir! Use this wood to make a statue for me, so that it resembles the Buddha's form.}

{align:justify|Then the divine craftsman addressed the king:}

{align:justify|- Though my craft is indeed foremost, when it comes to fashioning the Buddha's image, I can never exhaust its perfection.}

{align:justify|It is as if a man took charcoal to sketch the sun and claimed the likeness true — that would be absurd. So it is even with fashioning a Buddha statue from pure gold.}

{align:justify|There are heterodox teachers who commonly proclaim: “Brahmā can create the world[10],” yet even they could not fully fashion the fine marks of the Buddha's form. Only my craft is supreme in the world, and therefore I now fashion it for the king. This morning is the eighth day of the month, just when the Puṣya and Punarvasu stars appear. When the Buddha was newly born, these same signs of response appeared. Today is auspicious; let the work begin.”}

{align:justify|Having spoken, Viśvakarman took up his adze to hew the wood, and the sound of his hewing rose all the way to the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, reaching the Buddha. Through the Buddha's spiritual power, wherever the sound echoed, whatever beings heard the chisel-stroke had their defilements and afflictions wiped away.}

{align:justify|Then the Tathāgata smiled, in many ways praising the king's merit, and even from afar bestowed upon the king the prediction of supreme, right, and perfect awakening.}

{align:justify|At that time, the Lord of the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven addressed the Buddha:}

{align:justify|- World-Honored One! In the present world, has anyone in past kalpas ever made a Buddha statue?}

{align:justify|The Buddha told the heavenly lord Śakra:}

{align:justify|- All who ever made statues of Buddhas belong to the past, and have long since been liberated. Among this heavenly host here not one remains behind (stranded), let alone elsewhere. Only the son of Vaiśravaṇa of the North, named Na Lý Sa Bà, once long ago made a Bodhisattva statue; through that blessing he later became a king named Bimbisāra. And through meeting me, he has now been born in heaven with great power, departed from the evil paths for good.}

{align:justify|The bhikkhus Uruvilvā Kāśyapa, Gayā Kāśyapa, and Nadī Kāśyapa[11] — all three in past kalpas once repaired an old Buddha shrine, and through those causes have gained liberation forever. Gavāṃpati was once an ox; seeking grass and water, he wandered around a monastery, grazing among the bamboo groves, and upon seeing the Buddha's venerable countenance gave rise to a joyful heart — through that blessing he is now liberated. Śibi once held a jeweled parasol as an offering to a Buddha statue. Anuruddha lit a single lamp, also as an offering. Du Bệ Ma once swept a Buddha hall. A Bà Ma Na lit lamps before a Buddha statue to add brightness, and the bhikkhu Nanda, cherishing the venerable bearing, bathed it with perfumed water, and so on... Countless arhats such as these all once presented trifling gifts before Buddha statues. Down to the least, like Na Già Ba La, who before a statue's throne used a speck of red and gold pigment to paint a statue as an offering... Through such blessings all have left suffering behind and gained liberation.}

{align:justify|The Buddha continued:}

{align:justify|- Śakra, Lord of Gods! If anyone, while my teaching has not yet vanished[12], makes a Buddha statue, all shall gain liberation in Maitreya Buddha's first assembly.}

{align:justify|If any being, not for their own sake seeking escape, but wishing to realize supreme awakening, makes a Buddha statue, know that this is the true cause of the thirty-two marks, enabling them swiftly to become a Buddha. At that time, King Udayana pondered in his heart: “How can the statue I commission be swiftly completed?” Having thought so, he said to the divine craftsman:}

{align:justify|- Venerable Sir, strive with all your heart to bring the work swiftly to completion, that I may soon behold and venerate it.}

{align:justify|Then the god Viśvakarman displayed his utmost skill, wholly devoted and unflagging, and in less than a day the statue was complete! Seated cross-legged, seven cubits tall, its face, hands, and feet all purple-gold in color.}

{align:justify|Seeing the statue complete, its fine marks dignified and adorned, King Udayana's heart gave rise to pure, clear faith and he realized the acquiescence of conformity[13]. Having realized acquiescence, the king rejoiced all the more; all his karmic hindrances and sorrows melted away like mist before the risen sun — save for one karma still undergone in his present body[14], for having once spoken evil words against a holy person. The king then bestowed all manner of precious jewels and rarities upon the divine craftsman, but the craftsman addressed the king:}

{align:justify|- As the king makes this statue, my heart rejoices in sympathy; I vow to cultivate this blessing together with the king. What the king now grants, I dare not accept. If we would exchange gifts, pray wait for another auspicious day. Having spoken, that very night the god returned to heaven.}

{align:justify|Soon after, the kings of great lands, such as King Ajātaśatru[15]... Before this, all these kings had borne toward the Buddha the same thirsting admiration. Hearing that King Udayana's statue was complete, they all rejoiced and came together to King Udayana's place, each bringing quantities of flowers, incense, and music to offer to the Buddha statue, and presenting precious jewels to King Udayana. All the kings said: “Your deed is truly rare — it has drawn out the poisoned arrow of sorrow for us all.”}

{align:justify|Meanwhile, in the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven, the Tathāgata had expounded the sūtras and Dharma for his mother[16], and the gods all gained joyful benefit. What was to be done, the Buddha had done. The Buddha then told the heavenly hosts:}

{align:justify|- O young gods! The Buddhas, the World-Honored Ones, possess an ever-abiding body. If there are beings fit to be liberated, the Buddhas manifest themselves to teach and preach the Dharma to them. When the work is finished and none remain who can receive the teaching, the Tathāgata manifests no more. The unwise suppose the Buddha truly passes into extinction. But the body of the Tathāgatas is the Dharma-body, an eternal body, never truly extinguished.}

{align:justify|- O young gods! Such is the Dharma of all Buddhas: to liberate beings they appear — or appear not.}

{align:justify|Next the Buddha taught on, saying:}

{align:justify|- Know this! Among the gods here, those fit to be liberated I have liberated. Now I am about to descend to Jambudvīpa. You gods who think of me, be diligent and earnest; do not be heedless.}

{align:justify|Why? — For it is the offense of heedlessness that keeps you from the holy path of supreme awakening. Yet because in the distant past you planted wholesome roots, you now dwell here enjoying heavenly wealth and bliss[17], only to grow heedless, intoxicated with pleasure, cultivating no blessed deeds. These delights follow impermanence; once they fall away, you drift forever along the evil paths.}

{align:justify|- Further, O gods! Your afflictions are most heavy[18]: seeing any superior, you give rise to envy, never reflecting that whatever heaven's joys are higher come from creating more blessed karma — if I diligently cultivate, I too shall attain them. Now your forms are bright and lustrous as the sun risen at dawn; yet if you harbor envy and jealousy, you shall turn dark as spent ashes and cinders, and fall into pitch-black hells, unable even to see your own hand — thereafter to become hungry ghosts feeding on filth!}

{align:justify|- Further, O gods! Enjoying the blessed rewards of pure, dignified bodies and mighty, valiant power, through envy you shall take female bodies, utterly losing the virile strength and valor of true men.}

{align:justify|- O young gods! I recall that in ancient times countless kings suffered unjust ruin through your very envy.}

{align:justify|O young gods! Long ago there was an asura king[19] named Ô La, who practiced austerities with pure observance of precepts. You gods dispatched a celestial maiden named O Ba Thi to seduce the king's heart and make him lose his virtue. The king grew stained, his august virtue waned, and he was slain by Nārāyaṇa, while countless asura hosts perished with him. Having slain this king, Nārāyaṇa went on to exterminate the asura hosts and brought the maiden O Ba Thi back to the heavenly palace.}

{align:justify|- There was also a king named Na Ha Thọ, whom you gods beguiled with words into aiding the heavenly hosts against the asuras; once the asuras were broken, you gods turned and destroyed him!}

{align:justify|- Further, O gods! Over the Lady Śacī[20] you conceived jealousy; Śacī egged the king into quarrel, causing the sage A Gia Ba, though innocent, to be suspected and to utter a dreadful curse.}

{align:justify|- Further, O gods! You once deceived King Ế Trà, telling him: “Where those sages dwell there is much pure gold.” The king believed these words and forced the sages to yield it; the sages therefore conceived wrath, and at once fierce fire consumed the sage-king.}

{align:justify|- Long ago there was also a king named Đề Bà, who often held great assemblies to gain offerings; through this blessed karma his power was free and sovereign, and he ascended to this very heaven to enjoy heavenly pleasures. You gods, harboring envy, caused Đề Bà to fall from Trāyastriṃśa down to the human world, all his majesty shattered — like the moon gone dim, like a river run dry.}

{align:justify|- O young gods! In this world many possess august virtue and mastery, or attain supernormal powers; some accomplish the four bases of supernormal power[21]... yet should a single envious thought arise, such merit falls away all at once — as with Devadatta, who in dense delusion conceived envy toward me and at once lost his five supernormal powers.}

{align:justify|Then the Lord of Heaven addressed the Buddha:}

{align:justify|- World-Honored One! I now have a doubt I wish to ask about: what exactly is envy?}

{align:justify|Śakra asked on:}

{align:justify|- World-Honored One! If a being sees another's superiority and thinks: “How can I obtain what they have obtained” — is such a thought envy?}

{align:justify|The Buddha replied:}

{align:justify|- No! That is a covetous mind, not envy[22].}

{align:justify|- Śakra, Lord of Gods! Envy means seeking fame and gain for oneself, begrudging others their possession — hating and resenting what others have. That is envy.}

{align:justify|Then all the heavenly hosts rose from their seats, knelt with faces to the ground, joined their palms toward the Buddha, and addressed him:}

{align:justify|- As the Buddha teaches, we gods shall all obey. O Tathāgata, honored by the world, our Father, our Lord, most venerable, most victorious: toward us you have aroused great compassion, coming hither so that all the gods might benefit. Yet our wish remains unfulfilled; we would once more invite the Buddha in one request:}

{align:justify|- World-Honored One! People of the world below slight us gods. Why? Because all Buddhas are born among humans[23], and in the human realm attain right awakening. Among humans there are arhats who have realized the fruit. Solitary realizers of great august virtue also appear in the midst of humankind.}

{align:justify|If the Tathāgata now leaves this place to descend to Jambudvīpa, worldly people will all suppose that we gods do not know the Tathāgata's great august virtue, worthy of receiving the gods' rightful offerings, and will further suppose: “They do not know how to make offerings to the world's honored Tathāgatas. May the Tathāgata linger a little while to accept our small offerings, that worldly people may know the gods make offerings to the Buddha.”}

{align:justify|Then the Buddha silently accepted.}

{align:justify|The Buddha then told the venerable Mahāmaudgalyāyana:}

{align:justify|- Go swiftly ahead down to Jambudvīpa, inquire after the four assemblies, and speak thus: “All who remember me should gather at the land of Sāṃkāśya; within seven days they shall see me.”}

{align:justify|Maudgalyāyana bowed at the Buddha's feet, and in the blink of an eye arrived in Jambudvīpa[24], delivering the Buddha's message to the four assemblies. King Udayana and all the people, hearing the Buddha's tidings, leapt with joy in body and mind, all sorrow dispelled, all at ease.}

{align:justify|Then the four assemblies — bhikkhus and bhikkhunīs, laymen and laywomen — wishing to journey together to the land of Sāṃkāśya, first gathered in the city of Rājagṛha[25] and deliberated:}

{align:justify|- The Tathāgata, honored by the world, is about to descend to Jambudvīpa. Whoever first reverently venerates the Buddha shall be foremost during the time before the Dharma vanishes.}

{align:justify|The venerable Mahākātyāyana, hearing this, was inwardly displeased, fearing the bhikkhunīs might become foremost. Why? Among the whole order of nuns were Uu Ba Ban Da and Utpalavarṇā — two bhikkhunīs versed in the treasury of all Buddhas' Dharma, accomplished in supernormal powers, whom none but the venerable Mahāmaudgalyāyana could match. Reflecting so, Kātyāyana in every way rebuked the bhikkhunī assembly.}

{align:justify|Then the bhikkhunī Utpalavarṇā told the nuns:}

{align:justify|- We women, amid worldly life, are commonly held in honor: though our lineage be lowly, we are still revered by men and furnished with every provision. Moreover, the nuns in the Buddha's teaching mostly come of royal and ministerial kin. Our nuns are zealous, uphold the precepts, never violate decorum, and are replete with virtuous accomplishments — yet still we must bow to bhikkhus newly ordained. Now the venerable Kātyāyana rebukes us on many counts... For the nuns' sake I shall display skillful means, making the bhikkhunīs excel him. Having spoken so, she journeyed with the four assemblies[26] together to Sāṃkāśya.}

{align:justify|Then King Prasenajit, King Ajātaśatru, and King Nghiêm Xí of Vaiśālī... each king led four divisions of mighty troops[27] as escort, in great power. The elephants and horses they rode were adorned with precious things; banners, canopies, incense, flowers, and music lent them august and stately bearing, their aspect like that of gods — and all journeyed to the city of Sāṃkāśya.}

{align:justify|King Udayana then arrayed his four divisions as attendants. Mounted upon a white elephant caparisoned with jewels and embroidery, the king himself conveyed the Buddha statue he had made. Strewing flowers, bearing banners, with musicians playing in offering-train, he set out from his own land toward the city of Sāṃkāśya.}

{align:justify|Then the god Viśvakarman, together with the heavenly hosts, knowing the Buddha was about to descend to Jambudvīpa, fashioned three jeweled stairways from the city of Sāṃkāśya up to Trāyastriṃśa: the middle stair of beryl, the two side stairs of gold, the footrests laid with white silver, inlaid with the seven heavenly jewels.}

{align:justify|The heavenly lord Śakra sent messengers to the Yāma Heaven, the Tuṣita Heaven, the Nirmāṇarati Heaven, the Paranirmitavaśavartin Heaven, and the Brahmā Heavens[28], announcing: “The Buddha is about to descend to Jambudvīpa; if you wish to make offerings, come down to this place.” He further sent messengers to the Heaven of the Four Kings, the Dragon Kings of the great ocean, the king of Gandharvas, the king of Kiṃnaras, and the Yakṣa hosts, telling them: “Now the World-Honored One is about to descend to Jambudvīpa; bring your offerings hither.”}

{align:justify|Then the gods, dragons, spirits, and the rest, hearing the tidings, all gathered in Trāyastriṃśa.}

{align:justify|Then the World-Honored One, atop Mount Sumeru together with the vast hosts of gods from all heavens[29], was about to descend; all the gods filling the sky before and behind, their august virtue blazing, their radiance shining like the full moon amid space ringed with myriad stars, like the sun newly risen in dazzling brilliance. The Buddha and his assembly in that hour were just so.}

{align:justify|In that very hour, through the Buddha's spiritual power, five rarities appeared in Jambudvīpa:}

{align:justify|First, the gods there perceived none of the unclean things of the human realm.}

{align:justify|Second, women who saw the heavenly youths conceived no thought of lustful attachment.}

{align:justify|Third, men who saw the celestial maidens gave rise to no defiled thought.}

{align:justify|Fourth, earthly people from afar clearly beheld the offerings of the heavenly hosts.}

{align:justify|Fifth, the gods' bodies, bright, pure, and beautiful, are unseen by worldly people — yet through the Buddha's spiritual power, humans could see them with perfect clarity.}

{align:justify|When the Buddha stepped from the heavenly palace onto the jeweled middle stair, the god Brahmā stood at his right holding a white parasol, and Śakra at his left holding a white whisk. The remaining gods, abiding in empty space, followed the Buddha down, all sounding every kind of music. Gods bore gemmed banners and canopies, strewing flowers in offering. Gods of the Śuddhāvāsa heavens crowded the sky; hundreds of thousands of gods and goddesses wearing necklaces of jewels together sang the Buddha's holy virtues. Other gods in mid-sky scattered fragrant incense and sweet blossoms. The dragon spirits rained down light perfumed showers. The sky then was cloudless, and gentle thunder delighted all who heard. Gandharva and Kiṃnara spirits played the unsurpassed, exquisite music of the Deva-Nāgas, praising the Tathāgata's deeds of past lives.}

{align:justify|Then, in Jambudvīpa, kings, ministers, common folk, and the four assemblies filled the whole city of Sāṃkāśya: strewing fragrant flowers, bearing canopies, blowing conches, beating drums, all kinds of music, they offered reverence skyward. Hands raised, palms joined, they gazed up at the Buddha. Famous blossoms of heaven and earth, above and below mingling, fell thick to knee-deep; heterodox followers witnessing these things all gave rise to refuge and reverence[30].}

{align:justify|When the World-Honored One stepped upon the jeweled stair and had descended halfway, the Four Heavenly Kings at their stations arrayed offerings to the Buddha — offerings of supreme, exquisite rarity, unknown among humans since ancient times.}

{align:justify|The World-Honored One accepted the Four Heavenly Kings' offerings, then descended with the great assembly down every step to the lowest one. As the Buddha was about to set foot upon the earth, the bhikkhunī Utpalavarṇā transformed herself into a wheel-turning king[31], leading four divisions of troops and the seven treasures to the fore, swiftly descending from mid-sky to the Buddha's place. The kings of the lands all wondered: “Whence comes this wheel-turning king?”}

{align:justify|Then the venerable Subhūti, dwelling in his room, saw the Buddha descending and at once adjusted his robes to bow in reverence, while the bhikkhunī Utpalavarṇā shed her wheel-king body, resumed her former shape, and hastened to bow at the World-Honored One's feet.}

{align:justify|Then the Buddha roundly rebuked that bhikkhunī, telling her:}

{align:justify|- Do you not know? Subhūti venerated me first! Who told you to transform into a wheel-king? Admitted to the ranks of the renunciate and fully ordained, you have already received more than your share. Your wisdom is petty, yet your deceit is boundless; though you would repay kindness with compassion, it amounts to but a drop of dew. How could you become foremost in my Dharma?}

{align:justify|Hearing the Buddha's admonition, Utpalavarṇā was deeply ashamed, and addressed the Buddha:}

{align:justify|- World-Honored One! I now know my offenses are not few. From this day forth, I dare display supernormal powers no more.}

{align:justify|Then, in Jambudvīpa, the kings of the lands, the ministers, and the four assemblies all presented the offerings they had brought to the Buddha.}

{align:justify|King Udayana, bearing the Buddha statue upon his head[32] along with the finest rare offerings, approached the Tathāgata and presented them. The Buddha's body, replete with fine marks, stately, peerless, and radiant amid the gods, was like the full moon emerging from clouds. The carved Buddha statue, compared with the Buddha, was as an anthill beside Mount Sumeru — no comparison possible; only the curled hair and the gem-like tuft faintly resembled the Buddha, enough for the four assemblies to know it as A BUDDHA STATUE.}

{align:justify|King Udayana addressed the Buddha:}

{align:justify|- I bow to the World-Honored One! The Tathāgata in former times, amid birth and death, seeking awakening, practiced measureless, boundless hardships, difficult to practice, difficult to endure[33], and thereby attained a body most wondrous, beyond compare. The statue I made resembles the Buddha not at all; I venture to consider myself gravely at fault.}

{align:justify|The World-Honored One told King Udayana:}

{align:justify|- You incur no fault! You have now benefited the measureless, and none can compare with you. In my teaching you are now the first exemplar. Through these causes, countless beings of later ages shall give rise to the deepest faith. You have now gained measureless blessing and vast wholesome roots.}

{align:justify|Then the heavenly king Śakra addressed King Udayana:}

{align:justify|- O King, here and now have no fear. When the Tathāgata dwelt before in heaven and in this human world, he praised the merit of your statue-making, and the heavenly hosts greatly rejoiced in sympathy. Believers of later ages shall all, thanks to you, make Buddha statues and enjoy blessings. Now, King, rejoice and celebrate.}

{align:center|(END OF THE UPPER FASCICLE)}
