VIEN HOANG DAO

Vien Hoang Dao, styled Trung Lang, known as Layman Thach Dau, was a native of Gongan county, Hubei. His elder brother Tong Dao, styled Ba Tu, was known as Layman Huong Quang; his younger brother Trung Dao, styled Tieu Tu, was known as Layman Thuong Sanh. The three brothers, born of one mother, were famed in youth for literary talent, and grown up all delighted in Zen. Under the Ming, in the Wanli years, all three successively passed the metropolitan examinations. Ba Tu rose to Right Expositor-in-waiting; Tieu Tu rose to director in the Ministry of Rites. Later both brothers retired to their homeland, diligently worshipping morning and evening.

As for Trung Lang, that is Hoang Dao: after passing the examination he was appointed magistrate of Wujiang, where he judged lawsuits with great clarity and dispatch. When free from office he delighted in roaming among mountains and waters, and was later promoted to secretary in the Ministry of Rites before resigning on grounds of illness. The layman laid out a garden south of the city walls, planting ten thousand green willows. When the wind blew, standing on the high tower one saw the willow tips bend like a thousand waves, while orioles and other birds vied in song within. He called the scene "Willow Waves and Oriole Rivalry," and often roamed there with literati and Zen guests, sometimes drinking wine and chanting poems, sometimes discussing the Way. At first he studied Zen under Ly Trac Ngo, his understanding penetrating, his eloquence unhindered. But examining himself, he found that however far his subtle disquisitions ranged, they were only empty words of no real benefit, and so he turned toward the Pure Land, repenting and reciting morning and evening, and keeping the precepts. Ba Tu and Tieu Tu likewise resolved to recite the Buddha's name. The layman selected from the sutras and teachings and composed the Joint Treatise on the West. In it he discussed nature and characteristics to the point of perfect interfusion, entering deeply into the gate of nonduality. A few essential passages are briefly excerpted here: "Among the five kinds of practices, the first is the practice of faith. The sutra says: 'Faith is the first step into the Way, the mother of meritorious virtues.' Thus faith is the true cause of all practices. Even the perfect fulfillment of bodhi merely completes this root of faith — like a young bamboo shoot growing into a towering stalk, first and last a single root. No newly aspiration bodhisattva fails to succeed through the power of faith. In the Lotus school, faith is the very root. Then how should one believe?

First, believe that the fundamental wisdom and unmoving wisdom of Amitabha Buddha are not different from one's own. Like vast space: when the sun shines it is bright, when clouds gather it is dark, yet space itself has no nature of brightness or darkness. Understand, too, that sun and clouds lie nothing apart from the essence of space.

Second, believe that the practices Amitabha cultivated through countless kalpas, doing what is hard to do and enduring what is hard to endure, you too can do. Why? Because in countless past kalpas, while sunk in the three evil destinies, enduring immeasurable suffering of body and mind — sometimes wearing fur and horns, sometimes fallen amid boiling cauldrons and burning beds — such fruitless sufferings you have already undergone and endured; how much more, then, can you perform the bodhisattva's myriad practices for self and others?

Third, believe that Amitabha's immeasurable wisdom, spiritual powers, and fulfillment of immeasurable vows and works, you too shall attain. For within the Thus Come One's means of self-nature lie such inconceivable works in full. And because you and the Thus Come One share one and the same pure self-nature.

Fourth, believe that Amitabha neither comes nor goes, and you too neither come nor go; the Land of Bliss and the Saha are not separated by a hair's tip — wish to see, and you see. Why? Because both lands dwell within one undifferentiated true mind, here or there undivided. And because all Buddhas take the Dharma-nature as body and land.

Fifth, believe that Amitabha, practicing through immeasurable boundless kalpas until attaining the Way, never departs a single instant, and you too, without departing a single instant, stand level with the Buddhas. Why? Because time is illusory and unreal, gathered into falsely discriminating karma. Within the ocean of the Dharma-realm, the reality of karma cannot be found. To believe and understand thus is to enter the Way with a believing mind; it is the Pure Land faith-practice of all Buddhas.

Third is the practice of the six perfections. The Awakening of Faith Treatise says: 'The bodhisattva deeply understands that present practice is fundamentally apart from marks. Knowing the essence of the true mind is apart from greed and stinginess, he accordingly practices the perfection of giving. Knowing the essence of the true mind is undefiled, apart from the faults of the five desires, he accordingly practices the perfection of precept-keeping. Knowing the essence of the true mind has no marks of body and mind, apart from laziness, he accordingly practices the perfection of diligence. Knowing the essence of the true mind is tranquil, un рассеян, he accordingly practices the perfection of meditation. Knowing the essence of the true mind is luminous, apart from ignorance, he accordingly practices the perfection of wisdom.' Pure Land practitioners, in a single Buddha-recitation, fulfill all six gates. Thought after thought casting off is giving. Thought after thought pure is precept-keeping. Thought after thought continuing unbroken is diligence. Thought after thought gathered into oneness is meditation. Thought after thought luminous is wisdom. These six practices all flow from Buddha-recitation; principal and auxiliary are not two, phenomenon and principle are not different. Thus Buddha-recitation embraces all practices, for it is the One-Mind gate, and apart from mind there are no practices. To abandon the practices is to abandon mind; to abandon phenomena can never establish principle.'

Before long, Hoang Dao was recalled to his former post and gradually promoted to director of the honors bureau. Shortly after he again resigned on grounds of illness, returned home, and after a few days lodged at a monastery in Jingzhou city, where he devoted himself to recitation and passed away without illness.

Trung Dao, that is Tieu Tu: after retiring, morning and evening he diligently recited the Buddha's name. One night, the full moon of the tenth month in the Jiayin year, the forty-second year of Wanli, when his recitation was finished, he sat quietly and felt body and spirit lucid and clear. Suddenly, as though entering samadhi, his spirit rose above the house and, riding the clouds, flew away. On either side two youths supported him, saying: "Follow us!". They led him westward, swift as birds. Looking about, the moonlight shone clear, the lunar disk plain in the sky. Gazing below, mountains, lakes, fields, cities, and villages appeared tiny as mounds of earth, cups of water, swarms of bees, nests of ants. Sinking a little lower, he smelled a foul stench rising from below, and strove upward to the heights before he felt light and pure. Faster and faster, swift as lightning, they traveled; after a while the two youths cried: "Halt!", and together descended to the ground.

Tieu Tu looked about: the road ran straight as a stretched cord, the ground level, bright, moist, and lustrous, not of sand or stone. Along the road lay ponds more than ten fathoms wide, their steps of jeweled stone veined as if carved. In the ponds, five-colored lotuses bloomed in rivalry, of extraordinary fragrance and beauty. Along the banks stood rows of radiant trees, where strange birds sang together in harmony. At intervals golden bridges spanned the ponds as boundaries, their rails continuous as city walls. Beyond the trees on the far bank stood exquisite pavilions beyond compare. The people in the pavilions, pure and radiant as celestials, gazed at Tieu Tu and smiled. The two youths walked swiftly; the layman could not keep pace and called out: "Please wait a moment upon the bridge for me!". The youths consented, and he caught up with them and leaned together upon the bridge's jeweled rail to rest. Tieu Tu folded his hands and asked: "May I know who you are, and what place this is?". One youth answered: "We are attendants of Master Linh Hoa, who wishes to meet you and speak a little, and sent us to receive you.". The layman asked: "What manner of person is the Master?". The answer: "He is your elder brother, Vien Hoang Dao, styled Trung Lang. When you meet you will know; now we must hasten on!". Having spoken, they crossed the bridge and followed the road to a grove of more than a thousand great trees with emerald leaves and golden-petaled blossoms. Beyond the grove lay a great pond; crossing its bridge stood a gate of white jade, which one youth pushed open and entered first. The other led Tieu Tu through more than twenty tiers of pavilions, their golden hues dazzling, wondrous grasses and numinous flowers waving beside the steps. Arriving beneath a certain tower, a figure resembling Trung Lang, face bright as jade, robes like sunset clouds, over a fathom tall, came forward to welcome him, joyfully saying: "So you have come, brother!", and led him by the hand up the tower, where four or five figures like celestials sat around. Trung Lang said: "This is the borderland of the West. Practitioners of Buddha-recitation whose faith and understanding are immature and whose jewel of precepts is incomplete are mostly born here. ...

Beings of the ten directions who are reborn entrust their substance to those lotus blossoms; when the flower duly opens, they go each to his pavilion and gather with fellow pure practitioners of affinity. Since there are no beauties or alluring sounds, superior understanding is easily accomplished, and before long they advance to birth in the true land of the Land of Bliss! Tieu Tu thought to himself: 'A scene so beautiful, and yet still the borderland?' He asked: "Where have you been born, brother?". Trung Lang answered: "My pure vows were earnest, but defiled emotion was not yet extinct; at first I was born here for a while, but now I have reached the Pure Land. Yet because my understanding exceeded my precepts, I belong only to the earth-dwelling rank and may not dwell with the great bodhisattvas in the pavilions amid the sky; I must advance further. Fortunately, while in the Saha, thanks to sharp wisdom I composed the Joint Treatise on the West praising the Thus Come One's inconceivable merit in delivering beings, and so I have the reward of flying freely, roaming through Buddha-lands. Wherever the Buddhas preach the Dharma, I may go to hear — a truly excellent thing!"

Having spoken, he took Tieu Tu's hand and flew up into the sky, crossing a thousand myriad leagues in an instant, then alighted somewhere. Here there was no day or night, no sun or moon; the radiance was brilliant and unobstructed. The ground of lapis lazuli shone clear within and without; pillars and cords of gold and the seven treasures intersected, marking boundaries. The trees were all of sandalwood and auspicious kinds, row upon row facing one another, in tens of thousands of tiers. From every leaf sprang beautiful blossoms of wondrous hues and rare treasures. Beneath the trees lay jeweled ponds, their countless gentle waves spontaneously sounding the wondrous Dharma. The pond bottoms were laid purely with diamond sand. Within the ponds, precious lotuses of myriad colors emitted rays of varied hues. Along the rows of trees by the ponds, majestic pavilions wound in and out of sight, eaves jutting, pillars upholding curving roofs, great doors and latticed windows shedding light upon one another, great jeweled curtains hanging all about — every adornment complete. Around the pavilions hung countless musical instruments that sounded of themselves, performing the sounds of the Dharma. What is recorded in the Amitabha and Immeasurable Life sutras, compared with this, does not reach even one part in ten. Looking up, many pavilions floated amid the sky, wondrously fair among banks of sunset clouds. Trung Lang said: "What you see is the scene of earth-dwelling beings in the Pure Land. Beyond this lies the abode of the Dharma-body great bodhisattvas, whose scenery is a thousand myriad times more beautiful, and whose spiritual powers a thousand myriad times greater. By wisdom-power I may visit, but may not dwell there. Beyond that is the abode of the tenth-ground and equal-enlightenment bodhisattvas, which I cannot reach nor comprehend. Farther still is the realm of wondrous enlightenment, which only Buddha together with Buddha can behold!".

Having spoken, they flew together to another place, where the halls blazed with extraordinary light, of unknown substance. Beside this treasure, yellow gold and white jade seemed earthen in hue. Here there were no city walls, only encircling rails. The two sat together beneath a pavilion and conversed. Trung Lang said: "I never imagined such perfect beauty and joy! If while in the Saha I had kept the precepts more strictly, my bliss would not be only this! In general: first, when understanding exceeds precepts, the grade of birth is very high. Next, when precepts and understanding are equal, the birth is very secure. (Exceeding understanding: profound liberating insight. Equal precepts: strict keeping of the precepts.) If there is understanding without precepts, most are dragged by karma into birth among the eight classes of gods and ghosts. Fellow practitioners fallen into that state I have seen a great many; as for you, brother, your prajna disposition is deep but your power of precepts and meditation is weak. To awaken to the subtle truth without giving rise to precepts and meditation is mere mad wisdom! When you return to the Saha, while still strong you must truly awaken and truly practice, keep pure vows earnest, diligently practice skillful means to help others, pity all beings — and before long we shall meet again. If heedless and fallen into other paths, it will be truly dreadful! If you cannot yet keep the precepts fully, follow Master Nagarjuna's six fasting days. Among the precepts, the killing precept is most weighty. Convey my message to fellow practitioners: no one who daily wields the knife to kill, mouth greedy for fine flavors, has ever been reborn in the Land of Bliss! Though one may preach like raining flowers and rolling clouds, without true practice in earnest cultivation, it is all in vain! You and I, since the time of King Emptiness Buddha, have through many lives been brothers, and through our wandering in the six destinies ever so! Now by good fortune I have reached an excellent place; fearing you may fall into evil destinies, I have used spiritual powers and skillful means to bring you here and exhort you. Now the karmic conditions of the two realms, pure and defiled, differ, and you cannot stay long!".

Tieu Tu hastened to ask about the rebirth of Ba Tu and of departed fellow practitioners. Trung Lang answered: "The births of our brother Tong Dao and the others are all excellent; hereafter you will know of yourself!". Having spoken, he rose into the sky and flew away. Tieu Tu stood and strolled slowly along the pond bank enjoying the scene, when suddenly, as if slipping, he plunged into the water — and woke with a start. His whole body was bathed in sweat; turning, he saw the spent lamp still upon its stand, the bright moonlight still shining through the window — the fourth watch had already begun. He at once took brush and paper and recorded this "Journey to the Pure Land."

Earlier, Tong Dao had a son named Vien Dang, only thirteen, who fell mortally ill with congestion. The boy said to Hoang Dao: "I am about to die; uncle, how can you save me?". Trung Lang said: "Simply devote yourself to Buddha-recitation and you will surely be reborn in the Land of Bliss. This defiled land is not worth clinging to!". The boy at once joined his palms and recited Nam mô A Di Đà Phật without pause, while the family joined in support. After a moment he smiled and said: "I see a lotus, slightly red in hue.". Reciting a little longer, he said: "The flower grows steadily larger, fresh and bright in color, indescribably beautiful!". In a moment he said again: "The Buddha has come, of excellent marks and radiance, his tall body filling the whole house!". Presently his breathing grew labored. Tong Dao said: "Let the family support you; you need only utter the single word 'Buddha' at the very end.". Vien Dang uttered "Buddha" a few times, joined his palms, and passed away.
