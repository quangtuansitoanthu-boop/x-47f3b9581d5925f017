TITLE: 26. THE RETRIBUTION FOR TRADING IN LIVE ANIMALS (GECKOS)
TOMTAT: Mr. Ngo abandoned the gecko trade after watching To Vuong gut live geckos — and afterward To Vuong's whole family died horribly in a road accident, exactly as they had killed the geckos.
-----
{align:center|**THE RETRIBUTION FOR TRADING IN LIVE ANIMALS (GECKOS)**}

{align:justify|Mr. Ngo, an overseas Chinese in Thailand, traded in native produce. With little capital, he could only buy and promptly resell, so profits were small. Recently someone introduced him to buying geckos, most profitable. Yet Mr. Ngo worked at it only three months before quitting.}

{align:justify|His friends all felt the pity of it: meeting lucrative trade yet refusing to continue, content instead with thin-profit work like buying onions and garlic. Yet Mr. Ngo felt no regret at all — surely he had his reasons?}

{align:justify|Why is buying geckos so profitable? That begins with the dish "great tonic gecko wine." In the manuscripts of the famed physician Ly Thoi Tran of old it is written: "Gecko nourishes the lungs, boosts qi, nourishes the kidneys, enriches vital essence and blood, settles asthma, stops cough." Eastern medicine treats phlegm-asthma chiefly with gecko, and so the easy-selling market item is great tonic gecko wine.}

{align:justify|Geckos bought for brewing with medicine and soaking in wine — demand ever growing. Geckos were over-caught nearly to extinction. So pharmacies replaced geckos with mabuya lizards. Now in China mabuyas too are caught nearly to extinction. So pharmacies turned to buying geckos in Thailand.}

{align:justify|Northeastern Thai farmers in idle seasons gather to catch geckos to increase income. Some even abandon farming for gecko-catching riches — To Vuong's house the model.}

{align:justify|To Vuong's family — husband, wife, and one child — once most poor, hiring fields to farm. But since turning to gecko-catching, within three short years they owned a dozen-odd acres and bought a small truck. With profits so rich, the villagers all coveted it. And so the gecko-catching craze rose like a tidal wave.}

{align:justify|Yet a farmer catching daily can take at most a dozen-odd geckos. While To Vuong's wealth was plain to see — money in the bank besides. For besides catching geckos himself, To Vuong bought live geckos the villagers caught, dried them at home, then sold them on. The geckos dead by To Vuong family's hand numbered in the hundreds.}

{align:justify|Two reasons drove Mr. Ngo to quit the gecko trade:}

{align:justify|The first: one day, passing To Vuong's house by chance, Mr. Ngo witnessed To Vuong making dried geckos. Live geckos — caught and bought home — he slit with a knife, scooping the entrails... Yet the geckos were still not dead; To Vuong then drove a sharpened bamboo stick through the mouth straight to the tail, leaving them writhing in endless pain — dying with eyes wide open, staring fixed at their slayer. Then To Vuong laid the geckos out to sun-dry.}

{align:justify|Witnessing this cruelty firsthand, Mr. Ngo could not help shuddering; he turned and walked straight off, daring to look back no more.}

{align:justify|The second: in March of a certain year — Thailand's annual water festival, the whole nation at play — Mr. Ngo traveled from Chiangmai back to Bangkok. Crossing a mountain pass road, he witnessed a tragic accident. A small truck had overturned into the deep ravine by the road; at the scene lay three dead — To Vuong's family: husband, wife, and son.}

{align:justify|That day, though the water festival, To Vuong had not rested from work, still driving out to buy geckos. The whole family met disaster on the pass road; the laden truck overturned into the deep ravine. To Vuong's wife and child were flung from the truck, dead and mangled. Dying, their eyes still gaped wide and staring — like the dying geckos' eyes. More horrible than dare be seen was To Vuong himself: shot from the truck swift as a bullet, neither swerving left nor right, he fell precisely upon a sharpened bamboo stake road workers had just cut on the ravine-side below. To Vuong was pierced straight from mouth to anus — his wretched state exactly mirroring what Mr. Ngo had once watched him inflict upon the geckos.}

{align:justify|Seeing with his own eyes the whole family's death so wretched, so terrifying, his whole body broke in gooseflesh; he could only blurt: "Retribution! Truly retribution!" From then he wholly quit all trade touching the buying and selling of animals.}

{align:justify|COMMENTARY:}

{align:justify|The story THE RETRIBUTION FOR TRADING IN LIVE ANIMALS (GECKOS) is the most horrifying and plainest proof of the law of cause and effect, "as you sow, so shall you reap." To Vuong grew rich buying geckos — slitting living geckos' bellies, driving bamboo sticks from mouth to tail to sun-dry them: cruelty extreme. Retribution came in this very life: the whole family (husband, wife, son) died horribly in a road accident, exactly as they had killed the geckos (eyes staring wide, pierced by a sharp thing from mouth to anus). Mr. Ngo, witnessing both scenes (the slaughter and the crash), quit the trade at once. The story affirms: "Retribution! Truly retribution!" Whoever trades in live animals or slaughters, awaken soon: "Would you know the cause of a past life — look at the fruit reaped in this one." A minute of greed — traded for calamity upon the whole family. It is the strongest warning to all who still "profit upon beings' lives."}
