CHI THIEN

Zen Master Chi Thien lived under the Qing; his origins are not clearly known. During the Tongzhi and Guangxu years, he spread the teaching at Haihui Temple on Mount Lu. His virtue was lofty, and the four assemblies revered and gathered around him; he was a renowned worthy of Jiangxi in recent times.

After penetrating the mind-essence, he turned to Buddha-recitation, taking the Pure Land as the refuge both for his own practice and for his exhortation of others. Thus in those days the villagers of that mountain region all wore rosaries and every household enshrined the Buddha. From farmers and artisans to woodcutters and cowherds, when free from work many could be seen sitting beneath a tree or upon a rock, eyes closed, reciting the Buddha's name — a common sight no one found strange. That legacy is still handed down today.

In old age the Zen Master entrusted the temple to his senior disciple, Abbot Thanh Hu, and dwelled in a quiet hut devoted to practice. When students entered the hut to seek instruction, he bade them sit at ease and answered each according to capacity. There was a monk named Cam Phong, storekeeper in charge of the monastery's provisions, who each day also planted vegetables, chopped firewood, practiced meditation, and recited the Buddha's name; the assembly regarded him as an ordinary person. One year, at the beginning of winter, he came to the abbot's quarters to resign his post. Abbot Thanh Hu instructed him: "The monastery has just begun its Buddha-recitation retreat; do not leave so soon. Stay through the end of the year, then go!". He replied: "My time has come; I cannot wait!". The Abbot said: "I shall hold it back for you!". Cam Phong bowed in thanks and withdrew. In spring, on the third day of the new year, he came again to ask leave. The Abbot said: "If you leave at the new year, the assembly will be stirred; wait a few more days!". Cam Phong withdrew once more.

On the tenth day, he went to the abbot's quarters and firmly requested leave, saying: "I have lived long together with my fellow practitioners and feel deep affection for them. After the Lantern Festival, most of the monks will scatter to wander in all directions, and there will be no further chance to meet. So I wish my fellow companions to accompany me part of the way!". Hearing this, the Abbot consented. Cam Phong bowed in thanks, then went to pay respects to Master Chi Thien. The Zen Master, foreknowing, had prepared a cup of clear water and instructed his attendant: "If Storekeeper Cam comes, tell him to drink this cup of water; he need not come in for an audience.". The attendant conveyed the message, and Cam Phong reverently drank. At noon he still took the meal with the assembly. After the meal, the Abbot announced to the assembly: "Storekeeper Cam is about to journey far. Two hours after noon, I ask the whole assembly to don robes, bring small wooden bells and chimes, and come to his hut to see him off!". Hearing this, most were bewildered and puzzled. At the appointed hour, the precentor led the monks there. Cam Phong had already swept his hut clean and empty; in the center stood an incense altar with a Buddha image, with seats arranged on both sides for the assembly. When the Abbot arrived, Cam Phong bowed low, then prostrated before the monks. Abbot Thanh Hu kindly spoke a verse of Dharma entrustment. Only then did the assembly realize that the storekeeper was about to pass away to the West. After the Abbot withdrew, the precentor intoned the Amitabha Sutra, and Cam Phong, seated in his place, chanted along. When the chant passed to the hymn and turned to Buddha-recitation, after the incense had burned about an inch further, his voice grew fainter, and the rosary in his hand fell to the mat. The monk seated nearby felt for his breath and signaled that the storekeeper had breathed his last. The assembly dared not disturb him and continued reciting as before. After a while, Cam Phong suddenly opened his eyes and asked the monks to take turns in supporting recitation for a full day and night. Having spoken, he closed his eyes and departed directly.

In the Wuxu year of the Guangxu era, the court changed to new policies. Master Chi Thien lamented: "The age of the Dharma's decline has come, and I am old and cannot save it! I only wish to be swiftly reborn in the Land of Bliss, and after realizing the fruit of Non-arising, to return to this world to protect the true teaching!". Having spoken, he wrote farewell letters to eminent monks everywhere. Abbot Thanh Hu was then presiding over Yuantong Temple at Nanchang; receiving the letter, he hastened back. As the Abbot stepped into the monastery, Master Chi Thien was still seated leaning on his desk,
writing his final instructions. Seeing the Abbot, the Zen Master finished his business, smiled, and said: "Every great matter I entrust entirely to you. I am going!". Having spoken, seated upright upon his seat, he passed away.

MAC AM

Great Master Mac Am, Dharma name Thuong Nhan, styled Chan Nguyen, of the Zhou family, was a native of Hengzhou in Hunan. His father, a Confucian scholar, died early. As a boy the Master was bright and quick; a glance sufficed for him to read through several lines. At fifteen his literary talent far surpassed his companions, and his teacher thought his future official career would surely be brilliant. For the next two years he lodged at Yanfeng Temple to study, where, copying the Diamond Sutra, he came upon the line "the past, present, and future mind are all ungraspable" and suddenly gave rise to the thought of leaving the world.

In the seventh year of Xianfeng, when his mother sought to arrange a marriage for him, he secretly fled to Nanfeng Temple and left the household life under Zen Master Pho Chieu. The next year he received the precepts from Venerable Thuy Dinh at Phuoc Nghiem Temple. Hearing that Zen Master Chuc Thanh Luong's teaching flourished greatly, he went to receive the mind-seal. The following year he called upon Dharma Master Phap Van and penetrated deeply into the teachings, by which even the Confucian books became thoroughly clear to him. Renowned scholars who heard of the Master all delighted to seek his company. Vigorous in the upward path, he hid himself at Ky Cung Rock on the Southern Peak, where together with Dharma Master Thiem Van they encouraged one another in earnest practice.

In the Tongzhi years, the Master traveled to call upon eminent elders north and south, awakened to the source of mind, then returned to Phuoc Nghiem Temple to read the Great Canon. In the autumn of the second year of Guangxu, he built a hut beside Chuc Thanh Temple on the Southern Peak and cultivated in painful, secret effort; with time his realization grew ever deeper. From then on all secular books were thoroughly mastered, and famous scholars again sought his company as before. Dai Thien Temple on the Southern Peak, originally an ancient shrine, had been seized by others; the Master raised funds to redeem and restore it. Modeling its rule on that of Zen Master Che Wu, monks from the ten directions gathered like clouds until there was not room to contain them. All students there penetrated nature and characteristics and kept a dignified discipline. The Master used Tiantai doctrinal meditation as the vanguard for students, and Amitabha's Pure Land as the ultimate refuge. He himself kept a daily course of sixty thousand Buddha-recitations; in time he reached the state of recitation without reciting, so the sacred name was never interrupted.

In the spring of the Nham Dan year, the Master entrusted the temple to his foremost disciple, saying: "I am about to return to the West!". That winter he held a Buddha-recitation retreat. After little more than a week, in meditation he saw the water of merit glistening in the jeweled pond in splendor. Before long he felt slightly ill; he refused medicine, recited the Buddha's name with single mind, and had his disciples take turns in supporting recitation. He then gave up food and drink, lay in the auspicious posture on his right side, and maintained silent vajra recitation. The next day he saw Amitabha manifest, the white curl's light shining brilliantly; he at once rose, sat in lotus posture facing west, and told them to clear away the bedding. The attendant hesitated, objecting that the weather was too cold, but the Master said: "I am about to cast off a worn robe for a jeweled one, exchanging an illusory filthy body for a beautiful jeweled one!". When the monks heard and gathered, he calmly looked all around and slowly asked: "What is liberation?". They looked at one another, none understanding. The Master smiled and said: "In that case, better to learn from simple old country folk: keep a lifelong vegetarian diet and sincerely recite the Buddha's name — that is the surest of all!". He then had them strike the wooden bell for supporting recitation; after a moment, as the bell quickened, he bade them stop and instead
chant Nam mô A Di Đà Phật in unison. After some hundred utterances, the Master joined his palms and passed away. This was the thirteenth day of the twelfth month in the twenty-eighth year of Guangxu. Some hours later the assembly found the crown of his head still warm and his body soft and supple.

The Master lived sixty-four years, with forty-four monastic years.
