{align:justify|This startled and shook the Taiwanese boss—thanking the master unceasingly. His wife too burst crying, thanking mechanically over and over.}
{align:justify|The master told them:}
{align:justify|"Now the baby has stopped crying. From today onward, daily for the creatures you ate, diligently recite the Buddha's name delivering them. Nor is that all—hereafter release captive animals abundantly to dissolve old karma. Find time to visit Great Compassion Meditation Monastery inviting sutras home to read…"}
{align:justify|Husband and wife both promised surely to comply, diligently bowing and reciting…}
{align:justify|Two days later, Tiet Nhan with the couple came seeking Master Diệu Pháp to thank him. But the master had left the night before. I told them to offer gifts to Great Compassion Meditation Monastery.}
{align:justify|CAUSES OF MISFORTUNE}
{align:justify|One autumn day in 2001, a layperson led in a woman of about thirty, looking most haggard. She knelt before Master Diệu Pháp weeping, pouring out her misfortunes endlessly—forgetting dozens of guests present…}
{align:justify|"Master, I suffer so—begging Layman Hach to lead me here, asking your path of life. As a child I loved solitude, shunning crowds—playing alone, strolling alone… I lived most orderly, responsible… But at nineteen villains raped me—then I ached to suicide—so I left school a year. Two years later raped again… though villains were at last law-punished, my spirit nearly collapsed—studying no more. At twenty-five my parents set me socializing, introduced around… But my first boyfriend, near a year, cheated my love then left. Next year I met another—acting just like the first. I have nearly no faith left to live—misery, tears ever streaming. Please explain: why is my fate so unfortunate? Any way to change it?"}
{align:justify|The master invited her to sit. Musing a while, he asked:}
{align:justify|"Why wear no makeup today?"}
{align:justify|Hearing it she looked puzzled—then apologetically said:}
{align:justify|"Mrs. Hach told me… meeting you, wear no makeup…"}
{align:justify|The master said gravely:}
{align:justify|"I ask not blaming your bare face—but warning: this life's many misfortunes begin with your excessive makeup! Ever grooming beauty, clothes ever fashionable, fond of revealing, seductive dress… Though solitary and eccentric, you love all admiring gazes—loving to entice the opposite sex's eyes!… Am I right?"}
{align:justify|She nodded.}
{align:justify|The master went on:}
{align:justify|"Male-female desire—all share it—I blame nothing. But women are by nature weaker—so carefully guard yourselves. Chinese say: 'Adorning beauty invites lust; flaunting wealth invites robbers.' Meaning: wealth must hide skillfully—never flaunt! Craving to display riches makes yourself robbers' targets! Over-attending beauty, gorgeous makeup, or overly revealing seductive dress—stirring the opposite sex at a glance to lustful thoughts, blazing desire… they cannot self-control—naturally meaning to harm you. So, avowing yourself an (orderly, responsible) girl—you should skillfully guard yourself—never dress revealingly, stirring lustful minds, loving thoughts in the opposite sex…"}
{align:justify|As for spouses, all bear affinities, depending moreover on former lives' causes and karma. Past lives' 'who owes whom, who debts whom'… surely this life seeks out (meeting) collecting debts… Do you not see handsome youths wedding plain wives, or beauties wedding truly ugly husbands? So all good men and women in the world must never ape film stars' revealing fashions—for: 'Grooming, revealing invites lust; flaunting wealth invites robbers.'"}
{align:justify|If hereafter you wish a happy family, ever guard mind and will upright and pure, often reciting Avalokiteshvara (Guanyin) Bodhisattva—then though fate arranges 'evil-affinity spouses,' they may turn 'good-affinity spouses.' Do you hear and understand?"}
{align:justify|"Yes, clearly!"}
{align:justify|"Good! Act accordingly! Within a year, you shall meet your ideal lord. As for your former unfortunate fate—this still depends on your former lives' sown causes. Hence they say: 'To know past lives' causes, behold this life's fruits'… But from now on, keep the five precepts, practice the ten goods, read many sutras—gradually all shall clear."}
{align:justify|The woman gladly obeyed the master's teaching.}
{align:justify|KIM DOANH}
{align:justify|Kim Doanh, a girl of about thirty, tearfully told Master Diệu Pháp her misery:}
{align:justify|"At sixteen, seventeen I chanced upon 'Shakyamuni Buddha's Life'—at once unforgettable—loving it, unable to set it down. Then I read Buddhism's basic books—learning Buddhism's holy land Mount Wutai—ever dreaming, longing to climb it once—but graduating, then working—till twenty-seven the wish came true…"}
{align:justify|Just reaching Mount Wutai, Kim Doanh felt most familiar—indeed Mount Wutai was her homeland. Entering temples, lodging in guesthouses, meeting bhikkhunis—she felt them kin—settling, unwilling to leave—truly forgetting home—even forgetting her leave overstayed—till the nun reminded:}
{align:justify|"Are you not returning to work?"}
{align:justify|She woke with a start, remembering—hurrying home. Home, she learned the family nearly sent men to Mount Wutai seeking her—since leaving she never once telephoned—overstaying leave before creeping back—worrying her mother heart-ill. Family scolded her roundly—her mother declaring:}
{align:justify|"Henceforth go nowhere—unless you want me dead soon!"}
{align:justify|But that Mount Wutai visit left her longing ever after—even dreaming repeatedly of wearing monastic robes. Before long, she resolved to leave home—her mother fiercely opposing. She told her mother:}
{align:justify|"Mother believes in Buddhism too—is leaving home not good? Besides, brother, sister, nephews and nieces surround you—is that not enough? Barring my leaving home—do you know how I suffer?"}
{align:justify|Her mother flatly said:}
{align:justify|"Speak of this no more! Whoever wishes to leave home may—except you! While I live, never shall you leave home! Leave home, and I… die at once, watch!"}
{align:justify|No way left, she thought 'only imitating the Buddha—fleeing home to leave it'… quietly quitting her job, leaving a letter, secretly leaving home.}
{align:justify|She climbed Mount Wutai, entered a temple, begging the abbot to leave home. But the abbot told her first not to shave—dwell here practicing—see if monastic life suits? Barely half a month, her brother found her—telling how, her just gone, next day mother sent seekers at once—but none knew which temple—seeking continuously half a month—to now tracing her dwelling. Her brother added:}
{align:justify|"Mother, over your leaving home, has fallen gravely heart-ill, hospitalized—living or dying unknown…"}
{align:justify|So she must follow her brother back—mother just discharged, lying home healing.}
{align:justify|She lamented to the master:}
{align:justify|"Truly I 'leave' no 'home.' But my heart and mind remain at temples. Past thirty now—yet still unfree—hardly realizing my dream. Daily I live soulless… Please teach me—what shall I do?…"}
{align:justify|Miss Kim speaking, tears streaming, sobbing choked…}
{align:justify|The master, waiting her calm, said:}
{align:justify|"Have you not yet quit meat?"}
{align:justify|"Not yet."}
{align:justify|"Then begin quitting meat at once. Daily remember practice—morning-evening liturgies evenly. Though at work, hearts ever reciting the Buddha's name—memorize the 'Great Compassion Mantra,' the 'Heart Sutra,' or the 'Fourfold Pure Clear Instructions,' often upholding."}
{align:justify|Evening sutras done, sit forty minutes to an hour. Daily examine this life's ten-evil faults—resolving repentance. Evenly practicing so equals monastics—read many sutras—above all the 'Shurangama Sutra'—read much to grow wisdom.}
{align:justify|Dwelling home, you may still spread teachings benefiting beings—conditions permitting, saving others—never force! Buddhists must prize beings' benefit—how much more your mother! Never to satisfy your own dream harm her! Your leaving-home time falls about three years hence (if now you can let go of all clinging, vigorously practicing as I teach)—then surely hope exists…"}
{align:justify|Miss Kim, hearing the master's teaching, reverently obeyed.}
{align:justify|That evening near rest, I asked the master what affinities moved Miss Kim to leave home?}
{align:justify|The master told:}
{align:justify|"'A hundred years ago, a young man practiced the Daoist path on Mount Wutai—admired by an aristocrat's daughter. One day the lady with her maid visited the Daoist temple—bowing to the Daoist—offering a small cloth bag. The Daoist accepted—then opening, seeing silver within—deeply moved.}
{align:justify|So both sides came and went—gradually feelings grew… the Daoist, unable to withstand feminine temptation—at last wed the lady. Fearing gossip, the lady led her maid with her—husband and wife into green mountains and clear waters building a home. Married, the two lived happily, respecting like guests… The husband still loved practice—wife single-mindedly tending, caring—the husband's loving threads ever tangling… the lady's maid too stayed serving them to life's end.}
{align:justify|Miss Kim today is that Daoist's later incarnation. Deep love turned her female. Her mother is the aristocratic lady—while Kim Doanh's father is the maid. Formerly wholehearted, perfect in service—so this life reborn male—yet toward the lady and Daoist of old, still wholeheartedly tending'…"}
{align:justify|Hearing the master's instruction, I could only sigh—practitioners not cutting, ending loving clinging—at last hardly escaping rebirth.}
{align:justify|Truly:}
{align:justify|Practice is hard—hard as climbing heaven}
{align:justify|Teaching is arduous—as planting lotuses in fire}
{align:justify|Ending lust is hard—hard as cooking sand to rice}
{align:justify|So hearing teachings, believing, practicing, vigorously ending lust, removing love… is practitioners' root.}
{align:justify|DO SUTRA RECITERS STILL MEET CALAMITIES?}
{align:justify|Question: The "Ksitigarbha Sutra" teaches—picturing heaven, earth, ghosts and spirits ever guarding reciters, bowers to Buddhas—letting no fierce calamities enter doors, no water, fire, theft, robbery… occur—meaning all evil dissolves. But I see many lay reciters still meeting calamities: sickness, villains, car crashes, plane falls… myself often meeting unpleasing matters… I even see virtuous high monks and laypeople suddenly gravely ill, passing? Though never doubting the "Ksitigarbha Sutra's" descriptions, questions linger—please explain further.}
