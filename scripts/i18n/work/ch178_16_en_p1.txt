{align:justify|Vien Mau was the director of a metal workshop. Long ago, when he came to ask about his destiny, the God of Fortune answered:}
{align:justify|"Fifteen years from now you shall become a great wealthy merchant."}
{align:justify|The result: more than ten years later, Vien Mau's business failed and he had to close down; having borrowed far too much capital, debts piled mountain-high, and he fled abroad to escape his creditors. From then on he drifted in exile, unable ever to return home.}
{align:justify|Abroad, Vien Mau lived in great hardship. He peddled small goods with meager earnings, then hired himself out as a construction laborer—once the director of a factory, now clambering up and down strangers' rooftops; unskilled at the trade, he was dismissed. Vien Mau then took kitchen work, scraping by day to day.}
{align:justify|Later, hearing I had settled overseas, Vien Mau rode a freight truck to find me. He swayed atop the truck for three days and two nights before reaching me.}
{align:justify|Meeting him that morning, I started. The Vien Mau of old wore elegant Western suits, hair slicked glossy, stepping out to a polished car with driver and secretary in attendance… But the Vien Mau of today: grizzled hair, face lined with wrinkles, weathered and shabby, haggard and despondent, wretched and pitiable…}
{align:justify|I invited him in, poured tea, served refreshments—he looked as though he had eaten nothing.}
{align:justify|Vien Mau asked:}
{align:justify|"You said that fifteen years later I would be a great rich man—is this the time?"}
{align:justify|"How many years has it been, then?"}
{align:justify|Vien Mau answered:}
{align:justify|"Exactly fifteen!"}
{align:justify|Then he added accusingly:}
{align:justify|"Plainly you spoke wrong—your readings are worthless!…"}
{align:justify|"I…" (tongue-tied, words failing).}
{align:justify|Vien Mau's face showed aggrieved displeasure as he asked:}
{align:justify|"Why are your readings wrong, your words worthless?"}
{align:justify|"That is…"}
{align:justify|Vien Mau went on:}
{align:justify|"Long ago, when I was still a glorious director, I invited you to my plant to read the feng shui, design and correct every spot exactly as you taught… I also asked you to read my fortune—you affirmed that fifteen years later I would surely strike it rich, reaching the absolute pinnacle of success, the highest point of my life. Now it is exactly fifteen years—nothing in sight; instead, more wretched than ever! How do you answer?!"}
{align:justify|"I…" (sweating all over)…}
{align:justify|Vien Mau said:}
{align:justify|"Now I am at the end of my road—what do you say I should do?"}
{align:justify|Let me read for you again, shall I?…}
{align:justify|"Read again?… What good ever came of reading?" Vien Mau flared.}
{align:justify|I closed my eyes… and suddenly saw the God of Fortune, leading a small child by each hand…}
{align:justify|I asked: Who are these children?}
{align:justify|The God of Fortune answered:}
{align:justify|"Both are Vien Mau's children!"}
{align:justify|"Ah! I understand—for more than ten years Vien Mau has killed, aborted… hence these two infant ghosts."}
{align:justify|So I cried out:}
{align:justify|"Vien Mau, you are guilty of killing—your lover aborted two children…"}
{align:justify|Vien Mau answered:}
{align:justify|"True! But can abortion really be so grave an offense?"}
{align:justify|The God of Fortune appeared again, shaking his head at me; he raised a hand toward empty space, where a hermitage appeared, and out stepped a beautiful, graceful nun leading two small children by the hand…}
{align:justify|At that I was terrified (understanding the whole cause)…}
{align:justify|I said:}
{align:justify|"Vien Mau! You sowed seeds of a short life, committing the grave crime of defiling a monastic. This is why your blessings and rank collapsed and you sank into utter destitution! Those two children are hers, are they not?"}
{align:justify|Vien Mau's face turned ashen, sweat pouring over him! He stammered:}
{align:justify|"But… that nun loved me too!"}
{align:justify|"Heavens!" I sighed. "You must know this is a great taboo! Every monastery has precepts and Dharma protectors… monastics are those who have vowed to leave the mundane life for pure practice—if you go seducing a monastic, the offense grows by a degree. It was your unchecked conduct, daring to raise lust toward a nun until she bore two children, then having them aborted and killed… How enormous this sin is—your present dead end is of your own making!"}
{align:justify|"Is that so?"}
{align:justify|"Certainly!" I answered.}
{align:justify|"Then what must I do?"}
{align:justify|"You must vow to strictly keep the Buddha's prohibitive precepts, vowing to correct your faults and renew yourself from here on. Confess all your faults before the Buddha, sincerely bow in repentance again and again… and from now on, in every stirring thought, be careful and watchful, swearing never again to touch sexual misconduct. Forever cut off the very root of sin, act always openly and uprightly, and truly step onto the path of proper reform. Nor is that all: hereafter your mind must always hold wholesome thoughts, and at all times (by mouth or in writing) urge worldly people never to commit sexual misconduct, and never to raise wicked thoughts toward monastics. The sutras teach: 'One who keeps the precept against sexual misconduct sees the five blessings grow, and falls not into the three evil destinies'… You must strive in repentance, lifelong vegetarianism, diligent sutra recitation, doing good… only then may you redeem and eradicate your evil. Toward monastics especially, wholeheartedly support them in guarding their chaste, pure conduct. For the cultivator's goal is attaining the path, ending birth and escaping death; in the 'Four Teachings on Purity' of the Shurangama Sutra, the Buddha gave monastics most explicit instruction: 'They must cut off and eliminate the lustful mind!' This is the cultivator's first indispensable condition—the lustful mind must absolutely not be harbored. For nursing it, one shall surely stray onto the demons' path. Should you or anyone raise wicked thoughts to harass monastics, that is demonic conduct, and after death the retribution shall be utterly dreadful…"}
{align:justify|Hearing this, Vien Mau promised to comply. I gave him a few cautionary verses:}
{align:justify|One who has left the world to practice}
{align:justify|May not indulge in love's delights}
{align:justify|Wrecking a cultivator's precepts and conduct}
{align:justify|Denies the Buddha's house its purity}
{align:justify|The gods' eyes see to the very end}
{align:justify|Men and women in secret trysts}
{align:justify|Dreadful, harrowing evil retribution!}
{align:justify|Quickly reform—cut off wrong!}
{align:justify|I saw Vien Mau off, giving him two thousand dollars as capital, hoping he would henceforth resolve to practice, forever correcting his faults and renewing himself. Vien Mau's story reminds me of the film "The Thorn Birds," telling of a true-cultivating priest's love for a young girl, who toward the priest ever raised desire and a conquering heart…}
{align:justify|Today's films, books, and media love to trumpet tangled clergy-lay romances that never succeed, calling them beautiful loves—eager to screen, post, and publicize them… whether true or not, this corrupts morals and shames cultivation… greatly harming society's moral climate…}
{align:justify|A cultivator who violates lust must, by cause and effect, understand: the retribution is harrowing and dreadful. For having understood the teachings yet still offended.}
{align:justify|As for worldly people, the sutras say: "One who creates lust karma shall be repaid: spouse unfaithful, line cut off without descendants, after death fallen into the three evil destinies—becoming animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings—for hundreds of thousands of ages, hardly escaping, never human again"… These words are no scare tactics—they are entirely true!}
{align:justify|Not to speak of distant hell retribution—of flower-retribution alone: in this very life, violators of the lust precept lose position, ruin honor, squander wealth! The lustful suffer many ills, age early, live not long, are reviled by society, bind deep enmity, and end ruined in body and name!}
{align:justify|I think of the women and men who seduce monastics, and the tangled unwholesome ties between those within the faith, or between faith and world. Forbidden affairs that should never be… yet they exist. (Naturally, monastics are not yet sages—they are ordinary humans, not saints; who is without fault?) But let me remind you all: having resolved to practice, as monastics you must guard the precepts as your very life, understanding clearly the frightful, measureless retribution lust brings—this fault must never be taken lightly, never treated as jest or play!}
{align:justify|Remember these true stories I have told, and stay on guard. You must often repent your faults, cultivate diligently, and strictly keep the precepts to redeem your errant thoughts—only then can such sins be cut off.}
{align:justify|The lust precept is one of Buddhism's four grave precepts; saints and sages long ago recognized lust's misery, and so abandoned worldly pleasure to reach pure ultimate bliss.}
{align:justify|Toward wholesome books and scriptures that benefit human hearts and the world's morals, we should proclaim and support them, spreading them widely. Understand clearly: chasing bodily pleasure is vain—indulging lust falls into utter misery. Do not sport along in jest, nor madly sing its praises…}
{align:justify|Guarding a bright and pure heart helps society grow good and peaceful… if the moral climate stays sound, people's hearts grow honest. In all things, lead by personal example. Keep yourself chaste, your character bright as jade! Never do what harms body and mind. No calamity is more terrible than lustful defilement. Harboring no wicked thought is itself self-cultivation!}
{align:justify|Lien Sinh}
{align:justify|Translator's note. I was struck by the author's opening line: "In truth, at the start of this human life, our blessings and destiny may have been enormous… but through our poor understanding of cause and effect, we have: unwittingly wrecked our own blessings—through unvirtuous thoughts, words, and deeds"…}
{align:justify|In China, "fortune-telling physiognomists" swarm thick as flies! Naturally some are true, some false (though the false always outnumber the true). Even so, fortune-telling is so common in China that for a few coins anyone can have a whole life's destiny read.}
{align:justify|Finishing this story, I hesitated and wavered greatly! Not knowing whether to publish it or shelve it—for some details troubled me…}
{align:justify|The Buddha speaks of six realms: gods, humans, asuras, hells, hungry ghosts, animals. We see only the human and animal realms, so the other four grow remote… and among humankind, a few see through to the invisible realm—many see ghosts, but few see heavens, dragons, hells…}
{align:justify|Gods belong to the non-human realm: wholesome gods and evil gods. Wholesome gods usually promote goodness and uphold the Buddha's precepts. Evil gods answer the five desires and promote lustful deviance (for having created blessings and practiced, they too became gods).}
{align:justify|They say "Buddha and demon differ by a single thread." So close a distance means straying is easy—one slightly improper thought and you fall into the deviant realm at once!}
{align:justify|See the figures in these stories: richly blessed, yet one small slip, one slight offense, and the loss is beyond imagining!}
{align:justify|Vien Mau himself, offending, was punished in this very life—though only flower-retribution (bloomed, not yet fruited), it sent him plunging wretchedly. As for the nun who offended—how to explain her? One might say deviant and upright, Buddha and demon are hard to tell apart—so those poor in understanding cause and effect, lacking care, though wearing the Buddha's robe, easily stray and become demons' disciples unawares… If, offending, one knows the offense, sincerely repents in remorse, strives to cultivate virtue, and never repeats it, then the deviant can turn upright.}
{align:justify|In old temples two gods are often enshrined, called Mr. Good and Mr. Evil. Mr. Evil's face is fearsome, eyes glaring, tongue lolling… yet why is he enshrined? Because he sweeps away the deviant aiding the upright, cutting off evil to assist good.}
{align:justify|I pondered dozens of times, shelving the story, deliberating three or four months… then at last decided to publish, hoping: "Perhaps many, reading it, will 'rein in the horse at the abyss's edge' in time, will 'carefully guard virtue in mind and will… to enrich their own blessings!'?"…}
{align:justify|Reading this story does not mean that, hearing anyone claim godhood, you shut your eyes and hail them long live—you must think, testing their words with wisdom: do they accord with the Buddha's five precepts, with the laws the Buddha laid down? For the precepts the Buddha laid down were not to glorify Himself, but to protect people living happily, never violating cause and effect, never drawing dreadful retribution.}
{align:justify|Mr. Liao Fan of old, hearing physiognomists predict a miserable destiny, resolved to "live wholesomely, cultivate virtue, and turn his fate"—proving "destiny lies in one's own hands!"}
{align:justify|Having read this story, if you wake up, guarding body, speech, and mind, striving to found conduct and cultivate virtue—most precious. But if you merely wait on gods, hoping someone will pronounce your fate for you, believing blindly then drifting along, sorrowing and rejoicing at the "master's" words—that is most unwise!}
{align:justify|Some claim to be immortals and saints, boasting miracles—yet belong to deviant possessors—as the "Shurangama Sutra" describes (up to fifty kinds of skandha-demons!) To tell them apart is easy—for such deviants love slandering precepts, praising meat-eating, lust, indulging wine and flesh… ever exalting their egos, claiming sainthood, yet quick to anger—everything aimed at cheating money, seeking gain…}
{align:justify|Lien Sinh in this tale is kind-hearted—ever aiming to help people enrich virtue. He even spent his own money helping Vien Mau rebuild his life.}
{align:justify|I have witnessed many families enjoying utmost wealth, fortune, and power—yet merely for this lustful deviance falling into ruin—short-lived, houses wrecked, lines destroyed. Pray forgive me for faithfully translating and publishing this story without omitting any detail. May all cherish their present blessings and guard themselves well—so blessings and wisdom daily grow, conduct ever purer.}
