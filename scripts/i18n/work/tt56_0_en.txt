INTRODUCTION
{align:justify|Introduction}

{align:justify|Many people know that whoever sincerely makes a Buddha statue gains great merit, yet few know how vast that merit is. It is in this very SŪTRA ON MAKING BUDDHA IMAGES that the Buddha teaches the matter clearly.}

{align:justify|However, one must make the Buddha statue with a sincere heart, endowing it with fine and dignified auspicious marks. If complete perfection cannot be achieved, then at the very least it must display features expressing Great Wisdom, Great Compassion, and Great Liberation — or, as this sūtra teaches: it must possess the two marks, the white tuft between the eyebrows and the fleshy crown on the head, so that anyone who looks upon it knows it is a Buddha statue and readily gives rise to reverent faith; only then is there merit. Conversely, if a Buddha statue displays none of the Buddha's marks, so that onlookers cannot even tell it is a Buddha statue, no faithful confidence arises and it is even regarded with contempt, then such statue-making naturally brings no merit, and at times even incurs offense.}

{align:justify|Therefore, to help foster a correct and profound understanding of the merit of making Buddha statues, I praise and joyfully introduce to you, fellow Buddhists, this translation of the “SŪTRA ON THE MERIT OF MAKING BUDDHA IMAGES” by Bhikkhu Thích Thiện Thông.}

{align:right|FOREWORD}

{align:justify|Fashioning images of Buddhas or images of Bodhisattvas is a deed of noble meaning that creates a cause of immense merit and blessing! Thus for more than 2,500 years, since the Buddha entered nirvāṇa, his monastic and lay disciples — from India, China, and Japan to Buddhist lands the world over, including Vietnam — countless people have created images of the Buddhas and Bodhisattvas to behold, venerate, and make offerings to.}

{align:justify|Before briefly outlining the merit and blessings of making Buddha statues, let us travel back in time to learn where the making of Buddha images originated and who first resolved to make a Buddha image.}

{align:justify|By a fortunate chance granted to us: in the rains retreat of 1992, after translating the SŪTRA ON THE FIELD OF BLESSINGS OF ALL VIRTUES and the SŪTRA ON INCOMPARABILITY (sūtras on the merit of taking refuge in the Three Jewels), we came upon the MAHĀYĀNA SŪTRA ON THE MERIT OF MAKING BUDDHA IMAGES, a sūtra in two fascicles catalogued as number 694 in the Chinese Taishō Tripiṭaka. Having read it through, we were overjoyed, for we recognized novelties never before encountered in any Vietnamese translation of a Buddhist sūtra. Yet it may be said that in the book “Miraculous Responses of the Three Jewels,” Venerable Thích Thiền Tâm did translate a few brief passages touching on King Udayana's making of a Buddha statue.}

{align:justify|According to this MAHĀYĀNA SŪTRA ON THE MERIT OF MAKING BUDDHA IMAGES, when the Buddha was still in the world, King Udayana, ruler of the land of Kauśāmbī, was the first to use fragrant red sandalwood to fashion an image of Buddha Śākyamuni.}

{align:justify|What then prompted King Udayana to make a Buddha image? The motive that moved him was as follows:}

{align:justify|After nearly forty-nine years of teaching the Dharma to liberate beings, during one ninety-day rains retreat (likely his last), the Buddha was absent from the human world: he had ascended to the Trāyastriṃśa Heaven to preach the sūtras, convert the gods, and bring his mother, Lady Māyā, to attain sainthood. King Udayana could find the Buddha nowhere, and his longing heart ached with extreme yearning, until in grief and sorrow he neglected affairs of state for many days and cared nothing for royal pleasures. Not knowing how else to see the Buddha and ease his longing, he thought of making a Buddha statue.}

{align:justify|The king summoned the country's renowned sculptors to make a Buddha statue, but each declined, daring not to accept, for they thought: the Buddha's physical form is wondrously adorned with fine marks, and should the statue turn out flawed, they would incur offense and lose their good name. At that time the god Viśvakarman, traditionally said to be the god of craftsmen, transformed himself into a carpenter; bringing his tools, he undertook to fashion the Buddha's image to aid the king. Displaying his utmost skill, Viśvakarman completed the Buddha statue in a single day: seven cubits tall, purple-gold in color, complete with fine marks, so that whoever beheld it knew it was a Buddha statue.}

{align:justify|When King Udayana finished making the Buddha statue, its fame spread to neighboring lands, and the kings of great nations — King Prasenajit, King Ajātaśatru, and King Nghiêm Xí — all brought offerings to behold and venerate it, and all praised King Udayana's deed.}

{align:justify|When the three months ended and the Buddha descended again to Jambudvīpa, countless gods of the six heavens of the desire realm and the eighteen heavens of the form realm all presented offerings to the Buddha and escorted him back to the human world. Disciples monastic and lay, from at least four lands and every walk of life, gathered at the city of Sāṃkāśya to welcome the Buddha. A supremely magnificent scene, unprecedented in history, unfolded then — a divine meeting of Heaven and humankind, never before seen among mortals. All who sincerely brought offerings to the Buddha accomplished blessed merit and planted causes for liberation in this life and lives to come; many followers of heterodox sects thereby abandoned wrong paths for right ones and took refuge in the Three Jewels. Thus the Buddha fulfilled his original vow to liberate beings by appearing in this world. Hence the SŪTRA OF BEQUEATHED TEACHINGS records: “Those fit to be liberated I have liberated; those not yet liberated, I have already planted causes for their future liberation...” This clearly reveals the boundless compassion with which Buddha Śākyamuni, our World-Honored One, rescues sentient beings.}

{align:justify|So that beings of later ages may clearly understand the blessings and merit of making Buddha statues, the Bodhisattva Maitreya, on behalf of the whole assembly of the holy and the ordinary, asked the Buddha about the merit of King Udayana's statue-making, and the Buddha in turn spoke of the supreme rewards and merit of those who with sincere, pure faith fashion Buddha statues. This sūtra records those inconceivable rewards of merit in full. Below we quote a few passages to give you a general idea:}

{align:justify|“Maitreya! If someone embroiders a Buddha image with silk threads, or casts one in gold, silver, copper, iron, lead, or zinc, or carves one from fragrant sandalwood, or fashions one from pearls, shells, brocade and woven silks, red earth, cement, plaster, clay, or various woods... using such materials according to their means to make a Buddha statue, even one as small as a thumb, so that others who see it know it is a Buddha statue — that person's blessed reward I shall now describe:}

{align:justify|“Maitreya! Such persons, though still drifting through birth and death, will never be born into desperately poor families, nor into lowly or ignoble lineages, nor lonely and remote on a country's fringes. Nor will they be born into wrong-view families, traders' or merchants' homes, or butchers' households, down even to lowly lines, impure brothels, or the households of heterodox ascetics holding wrong views — unless through the power of compassionate vows; apart from that, they will never be born into such homes.}

{align:justify|“Such a person is often born into the clan of a mighty wheel-turning sage-king, or into a Brahmin line of pure conduct, or into a wealthy house free of grave offenses. Wherever born, they always meet Buddhas and reverently serve and make offerings to them; or they become kings who uphold the true Dharma, teaching it to others without going against the path, or mighty wheel-turning sage-kings. Wherever born, they are always men, never taking a woman's body. Nor do they take the base body of a hermaphrodite or one of dual sex; the body they receive at birth is never ugly. Their eyes are never blind or defective, their ears never deaf or misshapen, their noses never crooked or broken, their mouths never twisted, their lips never drooping nor pursed, their teeth never gapped, missing, black, or yellow, their tongues never short or drawn back, their necks never goitered, their backs never hunched, their skin never blotchy, their hands never stubby, their feet never lame — never too gaunt, never too stout, neither too tall nor too short — none of the unlovely features at all...”}

{align:justify|The above is but a small excerpt on the rewards of lineage and bodily form for makers of Buddha statues. Beyond these blessings there are further fruits: the eradication of all manner of karmic hindrances, such as the five heinous transgressions, the ten unwholesome deeds, the four grave violations, and so forth — in short, the heaviest evil karma a human being can incur. Yet if one sincerely resolves to make a statue, repents, and vows never to repeat the offense, those evil deeds and their fruits are all destroyed.}

{align:justify|Why is the merit of making statues so great?}

{align:justify|Because all Tathāgatas, from the time they first aroused the aspiration to practice until attaining Buddhahood, passed through countless asaṃkhyeya kalpas, accumulating measureless blessings and accomplishing boundless merit and wisdom, and with that merit they ever protect sentient beings of pure faith. That is why making Buddha statues can wipe away the gravest evil karma.}

{align:justify|Moreover, the power of mind in each sentient being — in each of us — is itself inconceivable, inherently replete with Ganges'-sands of merit and wondrous functions. If we constantly contemplate the Buddhas' meritorious virtues, constantly regard the Tathāgatas as endowed with the thirty-two fine marks and eighty beauties, the ten powers, the four fearlessnesses, the six perfections, and great kindness, great compassion, and great wisdom, then in the very act of contemplating the Buddhas' virtues we activate the seeds of our own inconceivable meritorious cause. Through the power of this perfuming, even the heaviest evil karma and adverse fruits can be transformed. We contemplate the fine marks upon our own physical bodies; once the seed exists, the fruit will arise, and when the fruit arises we shall possess those fine marks. This principle is utterly true and never fails.}

{align:justify|In short, by contemplating the Buddhas' virtues externally, we accomplish the merit of the Buddha-mind within our own minds; and by contemplating the Buddha's virtues within our minds, we accomplish blessed rewards of body and circumstance externally. This is a wondrous principle called the Great Vehicle, and the making of Buddha statues belongs to that wondrous principle — so much so that the sūtra on statue-making is itself called the MAHĀYĀNA SŪTRA ON THE MERIT OF MAKING BUDDHA IMAGES.}

{align:justify|This sūtra comprises two fascicles, composed in a sharp, outstanding literary style. Beyond teaching the merit and blessings accomplished through statue-making, it also clearly reveals the Buddha's supremely skillful means: how, to teach beings, he manifests undergoing hindrances and hardships. Apart from the most learned, few among us Buddhists know of this.}

{align:justify|The sūtra also clearly teaches the causes by which beings must take female bodies, hermaphrodite or dual-sex bodies, be born in times or places without the Buddhadharma, or be born in wild borderlands — truly matters hard to encounter and hard to understand.}

{align:justify|Seeing that this sūtra holds such great value, we have endeavored to translate it into Vietnamese to offer to our present age and to contribute our small share to the Vietnamese translation of the Tripiṭaka undertaken by Vietnamese Buddhism.}

{align:justify|Moreover, since a sūtra of such literary and doctrinal worth also contains a rich treasury of Sino-Vietnamese terms and expressions that may aid those studying literary Chinese today, we present both the Chinese-character text and its Vietnamese reading, for the convenience of those who delight in chanting it.}

{align:justify|Though we have tried our best, our work and understanding remain shallow and flawed in many places; yet as we have understood, so have we recorded it, and we sincerely hope the learned will instruct us.}
