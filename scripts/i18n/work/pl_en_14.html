PHAM NGUYEN LE

Layman Pham Nguyen Le, styled Dung Hoa, lived under the Qing, a native of Qiantang. In youth he studied Confucianism, upright in conduct, serving his parents with great filial devotion. Once, when his father was ill, he cut flesh from his thigh, brewed it with medicine, and offered it; the illness was cured. When his mother, nee Du, fell gravely ill, he did the same once more, and she likewise improved.

Before long both parents passed away, and his wife followed soon after. Then in his prime, Nguyen Le, moved by life's impermanence, cast off worldly ties and followed a recluse to study the Way of immortals. After more than ten years, his effort showed some effect. Later, reading Master Van The's books, he received the Three Refuges and five precepts. Nguyen Le kept the precepts firmly: garments of silk or fur he would not use. Abandoning his former practice, he devoted himself to the Pure Land, cultivating the contemplative gate with special precision. Earlier, when his mother lay dying with clouded spirit, the layman had urged her to contemplate Avalokiteshvara with single mind, and she did so. A few days later she suddenly said: "The Bodhisattva has escorted Mother to a strange, wondrous, bright realm!", and passed away joyfully. For this reason Nguyen Le greatly rejoiced in the efficacy of contemplation.

Gentle by nature, the layman delighted in releasing life, giving alms, and aiding the poor, sick, and afflicted. In charitable works he was diligent and thorough, sparing no toil. Meeting monks and nuns, he offered support according to his means, omitting none. Thus in his time, hearing Nguyen Le's name, monastics and laity alike knew and esteemed him.

In the summer of the eighth year of Daoguang, the layman sealed himself in retreat at Dai Dong Pavilion south of the city, fixing a hundred-day term of Buddha-recitation. One day in quiet sitting, the mysterious powers of his former immortal practice suddenly manifested. He felt the pure energies of heaven and earth gather from midair in many streams, entering through crown, nose, and mouth straight down to the elixir field, merging with his own original energy. Body and mind were then most peaceful, light, and blissful beyond compare. In a moment an infant some inches tall emerged from the crown gate, flew sporting about for a time, then re-entered through the crown. From then on, whenever practice reached utter stillness and forgetfulness of worldly thoughts, the infant would come and go as before. At first Nguyen Le's heart rejoiced; then he reflected: "Is this one of the fifty kinds of skandha-maras spoken of in the Shurangama Sutra? If I take it for a marvel, I shall surely fall among deviants. Moreover, Buddha-reciters aspire to the West; the holy scenes do not appear — what use is this?". So he constantly maintained illuminating awareness and right mindfulness, and the infant appeared no more. Through this condition he deeply awakened to the truth of mind-only, and his faith, vows, and practice grew ever more pure and firm. Later he often told others: "That state was the most perilous pass of my life. Had I not wakened early, I would have fallen into side gates. Thus know that practice requires vigilance and heedfulness!".

In the summer of the eleventh year of Daoguang, Nguyen Le convalesced at Tich Chieu Temple outside Thanh Thai Gate. — In that district lived a Mrs. Tram, blind in both eyes. She aspired to Buddha-recitation but was often hindered by her daughter-in-law. Hearing of it, the layman said: "This can be settled well. The daughter-in-law acts so because the household is poor; aid their livelihood and all will be well.". He went into the city to solicit help, securing for Mrs. Tram several pecks of rice monthly. With the surplus she shared with her daughter-in-law, who rejoiced and hindered no more. Nguyen Le then explained the benefits of Buddha-recitation and taught repentance and vows. Months later, a neighbor of the Trams told the layman: "The old lady who recited has now been reborn!". Asked how, the neighbor recounted: how diligently she recited after his aid, how she repented and vowed; that in the past month Mrs. Tram foreknew her western date and told her daughter-in-law; that at the appointed time she bathed, changed robes, sat with joined palms reciting, and passed away. Mrs. Tram left word asking the daughter-in-law to convey thanks to Master Pham. But she had just passed away with no coffin or burial goods, and the daughter-in-law meant to sell her little daughter to provide them. What was to be done?

Hearing this, Nguyen Le consulted good friends, jointly contributed a sum, and had the neighbor carry it to the daughter-in-law. A week later he visited to inquire. Arriving, he found the daughter-in-law rosary in hand, lips softly reciting. Seeing the layman, she joyfully welcomed and thanked him. Once seated, neighbors hearing the news gathered, vying to see Master Pham. One after another they retold Mrs. Tram's story, much as the neighbor had told it. They pointed to a little girl, saying: "This is the child who owes you thanks for not being sold!". Nguyen Le asked where Mrs. Tram's coffin lay; the daughter-in-law said: "When the coffin and burial were bought, the neighbors said: Master Pham, a city man, yet labors from afar in good works — we as neighbors should not stand idly by! So all contributed to buy land, and Mother's coffin, together with my husband's long-enshrined coffin, were buried on the same day!". The layman heard it and returned joyfully.

That winter, Nguyen Le settled and handed over his unfinished charities to another fellow practitioner. In the first month of the next year, he visited each fellow practitioner's home in turn, earnestly urging practice, like one about to journey far. His son had business in Tung Giang; he said: "Going abroad, handle all matters well. Your father is old; should any change befall, do not be alarmed. Keep to your duty toward the charges entrusted to you!". The young man understood, but took it only for an elder's excessive caution.

On the twenty-fourth day of the first month, Nguyen Le rose early and told his family: "Today I feel slightly weary!". He still took morning gruel as usual. Finished, he strolled before a neighboring house, stood musing awhile, returned, and sat upright upon his bed reciting. His children, finding it strange, asked; he waved them to silence. At noon his reciting faded and he peacefully passed away. At the You hour next day, at encoffining, the crown of his head was still warm. This was the twelfth year of Daoguang; the layman lived sixty-three.

A comment: See how Dung Hoa settled all matters, bade friends farewell, and instructed his son — it cannot be called unforeknowledge of the end. But why did he not declare it plainly? In my humble view, foreknowledge of the hour is precious for settling one's own mind in firmness, not for display before others. How much more so when, if good friends all gather, there may be benefit aiding the Way — yet should relatives, unable to restrain worldly feeling, crowd about weeping, would not success turn to failure? Layman Dung Hoa's silence surely sprang from this reason.
