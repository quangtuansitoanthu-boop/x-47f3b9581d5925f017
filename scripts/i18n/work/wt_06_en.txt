TITLE: 07. WHATEVER THE FACTORY HAS, MY HOME HAS
TOMTAT: Mr. Hong, a machinist in a state machine works, suffered severe back pain from stealing state property (iron, nails, screws...) to take home. Meeting Master Miaofa, he repented, repaid through devoted service, and recovered.
-----
{align:center|**WHATEVER THE FACTORY HAS, MY HOME HAS**}

{align:justify|Mr. Hong, past 50, was a machinist in a government machine works. One afternoon he came with an elderly layman to honor Master Miaofa. The moment he entered he laughed heartily, joined his palms in greeting, and bowed respectfully three times before the Master; without waiting to be invited to sit, he sat down of his own accord. One glance told his refreshingly open character.}

{align:justify|Master Miaofa asked him:}

{align:justify|"Is it not your aching back that brings you here?"}

{align:justify|Somewhat startled, he said:}

{align:justify|"Master, you are truly miraculous — I said nothing and you know all." Then he rose, lifted his shirt to show his back:}

{align:justify|"Master, look — my back brace!"}

{align:justify|The belt he wore was a loop of tire inner-tube fashioned into a brace, some 15cm wide. Sitting again, he continued:}

{align:justify|"I have worn this tube brace for nearly 10 years. The doctor says my back muscles are worn out — medicines and injections all useless. I can only rely on this homemade belt; without its protection, I can hardly straighten my back! It all comes from overwork in my youth..."}

{align:justify|Pausing a moment, he went on:}

{align:justify|"A friend told me you are a living Bodhisattva. Please save me. Heal my back, and every day I shall burn incense to you — even a hundred prostrations a day, I shall do them." With that, he joined his palms to the Master again.}

{align:justify|Master Miaofa smiled and said:}

{align:justify|"First, I am no Bodhisattva. Second, I am no healer of illness. If I were a Bodhisattva, could we still sit here talking together? And if I could heal illness, I would go work as a physician in a hospital. In truth, whether your back heals or not is decided by you yourself. For the one who tied the bell must untie it. Now I ask you: if I told you to give up smoking, drinking, stealing, and meat-eating — could you do it?"}

{align:justify|"So my illness may heal, I shall keep whatever precepts you name — but I am no thief or robber!"}

{align:justify|"Besides the pain in your back, do you sometimes feel a most heavy pressure upon it?"}

{align:justify|"Yes! As though dozens of kilos weighed on my back."}

{align:justify|"Have you taken from the works such things as iron, hinges, nails, nuts, screws...? At times carrying whole cases home?"}

{align:justify|Mr. Hong sat stunned at these words, then after a while nodded:}

{align:justify|"Truly there is such a thing! I am a fitter in the works — 'he who stands by the storied eaves first enjoys the moon.' These days, who does not take a little state property for himself? Can that be called stealing? Even our works manager takes things home!" — Mr. Hong argued in self-defense.}

{align:justify|"Carrying state property home as your own — if that is not stealing, what is? Did you openly carry things home? Or did you stealthily slip them into an empty lunch tin, glance around shiftily... and smuggle them out of the works? Was it so?" — the Master pressed sternly.}

{align:justify|The Master's question left Mr. Hong staring wide-eyed at him, speechless.}

{align:justify|The Master continued:}

{align:justify|"What is more, you took pliers, screwdrivers, wire, and workshop steel home to fashion household goods: chimney pipes, dustpans, stoves, chopsticks, tongs..."}

{align:justify|Mr. Hong suddenly interrupted the Master:}

{align:justify|"Master, how do you know all this?"}

{align:justify|"Why, it is they that press upon your back so you cannot straighten up!"}

{align:justify|Suddenly the study felt like an interrogation room; the hearty vigor with which Mr. Hong had entered turned to dejected gloom. Straining pitifully, he said in a small voice:}

{align:justify|"What I took home was never for my own use alone, nor did I sell it — most went to relatives and neighbors. Workers' wages are low; taking state property is what everyone does, the storekeeper included. It is a matter of mutual sympathy and help — an 'unwritten rule' in the works. I never knew it was stealing. Just now the Master's words struck like thunder in a clear sky, shaking my heart awake. Truly heaven has eyes! Everything I secretly took home, he knows to the last. And now it all weighs upon my back. This is truly 'evil begets evil retribution.' All these years my illness has grown worse — it turns out the stolen goods grew ever more, piling up. Only today is it clear: 'Would you keep it unknown — then do it not.' How true that is! I assure the Master: from this day I resolve to strictly keep your conditions — no smoking, no drinking, no meat, no stealing of state property. Tomorrow I shall invite a Buddha statue home to worship. I so regret coming to the Buddha Dharma this late. Else I would never have done wrong. May I ask: which Buddhist books should I read?"}

{align:justify|I had never expected Mr. Hong's awakened mind to be so keen; hearing his repentance, my heart brimmed with Dharma joy. The Master seemed glad as well, so I said:}

{align:justify|"I shall give you some Buddhist books to read first; afterward you may go to a pagoda and request sutras to study."}

{align:justify|Mr. Hong thanked me, then asked the Master again:}

{align:justify|"The things I formerly took from the works — may I convert them to money and pay it back?"}

{align:justify|The Master said:}

{align:justify|"If you repay it that way, your troubles will only grow greater. Now that your heart knows its fault and repents, the offense is thereby dissolved. If you wish to repay the works, you may 'redeem fault with merit' — strive to do much devoted service for the works to compensate, and explain to your friends so they avoid this wrong and no longer covet state property as their own. You must make your own person the example, keeping your conduct noble and pure. This is knowing fault and repenting — known, it must be practiced. Surely merit can repay the old offense."}

{align:justify|Leaving, Mr. Hong solemnly accepted the Buddhist books and said reverently to the Master:}

{align:justify|"Only wait and see — if I do not know repentance, I shall be most unworthy of you!"}

{align:justify|Half a month later Mr. Hong came to my place; entering, he at once prostrated before the Buddha statue in the house. Rising, he lifted his clothes to show me — the back brace was gone.}

{align:justify|He beamed:}

{align:justify|"Since that day home, I have felt more than half healed. All the Master required, I have obeyed. I have set up a Buddhist altar and a sutra cabinet, invited Guanyin home, morning and evening burning incense in worship. Every day I read the sutras — how wonderfully the Buddha's sutras teach! Now my heart is at peace, my breath harmonious, my illness healed. These days my whole family rejoices as at New Year. How I thank the Master!"}

{align:justify|Hearing this, my heart rejoiced with his. Brother Hong is truly proof of "lay down the butcher's knife and at once become a Buddha!"}

{align:justify|COMMENTARY:}

{align:justify|The story WHATEVER THE FACTORY HAS, MY HOME HAS is a down-to-earth lesson in "greedily stealing public property — retribution in a chronically aching back." Mr. Hong carried steel, nails, nuts, screws, pliers, screwdrivers... home from the works to fashion household goods, and they "weighed upon his back" until he wore a tire-tube brace for 10 years. With sincere repentance, vowing to steal no more, setting up a Buddha altar, and "redeeming fault with merit" through service to the works, his illness healed. The story stresses: "Would you keep it unknown — then do it not." However "unwritten" in office or workshop, taking state property home is still theft, and retribution will fall upon the body. It is a warning to all with "light fingers" toward public property: sooner or later repayment is due — and the best way is repentance, practice, and compensation through merit.}
