TU NGAN

Venerable Tu Ngan, styled Nguyen Dang, of the Ta family, was a native of Qiantang at Hangzhou. In youth he studied Confucianism and ranked among the licentiates. Before long, feeling the impermanence of worldly affairs, he took refuge in the Three Jewels and traveled to study under good spiritual teachers of both Zen and the doctrinal schools. Later, hearing Dharma Master Ngoc Phong teach: "To seek escape from suffering, there is no easier gate to accomplishment than Buddha-recitation," he resolved to recite sixty thousand utterances of the Buddha's name daily as his fixed course. Whatever merit he made in life, he dedicated toward the Pure Land. In the Renshen year of the Tongzhi era, he further resolved to receive the five precepts at Haichao Temple.

In the ninth year of Guangxu, weary of the dusty world and longing for early liberation, he entrusted his household to his two sons and went straight to Huguo Temple, where he asked Abbot Kinh Phong to shave his head. That winter he received the complete precepts at Qiyuan Temple on Zhong Mountain. He then returned to Huguo Temple to cultivate the pure karma with single purpose. Through long and exhausting effort, Tu Ngan gradually fell ill; though treated many times, the illness would heal only to return. His two sons, missing him dearly, brought him home to convalesce, setting aside a quiet room as his place of secluded practice. In the autumn of the fifteenth year of Guangxu, he suddenly contracted dysentery, and medicine proved ineffective. From then on, though his food and drink steadily diminished, his recitation grew ever more reverent and earnest. On the tenth day of the ninth month, Tu Ngan called his two sons and said: "The hour of my rebirth has come. Go and invite the monks to come to the house and recite the Buddha's name for seven days, to escort me to the West!". He fixed the early morning of the twelfth as the start of the seven-day retreat, telling the monk Lang Phong that he would remain only seven more days. On the full-moon day, taking the hand of his Dharma friend Dinh Son, he said: "Only three days more, and I shall return to the Land of Bliss!".

On the night of the seventeenth, in the fourth watch, he asked the hour; his sons answered that it was the Chou hour. Tu Ngan said: "The Chou hour means the eighteenth day has already begun — the appointed date of my rebirth. Invite the monks to chant the sacred name in support!". As the assembly voiced the Buddha's name, he recited along. After about half a stick of incense, he suddenly fell silent and closed his eyes. After a while he abruptly opened his eyes, joined his palms, thanked the monks, and said: "I have already reached the West and paid homage to Amitabha and the two great bodhisattvas. The Buddha in his compassion bestowed a pure robe upon me, and Avalokiteshvara Bodhisattva with a willow branch sprinkled sweet dew upon my head. Gazing afar, I saw Great Master Lianchi expounding the Dharma to the assembly of superior good persons. Roaming over the vast jeweled pond, looking across the precious lotus blossoms, I saw one lotus throne inscribed with the name of my own rebirth. The Buddha told me to return temporarily to the Saha world and announce to everyone that I have been reborn in the Pure Land. I earnestly urge you all to practice Buddha-recitation diligently, so that one day we may meet together in the Lotus Land!". Having spoken, he asked for cool water, drank three sips, lay down on his right side, recited the Buddha's name some dozens of times, and passed away.

The next day, at the encoffining, the crown of his head was still warm.
